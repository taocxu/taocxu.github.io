The Capability Paradox of Industrial Policy: Accumulation, Erosion, and Political Choice

Research Note · 24 November 2026

1. Why Capabilities Evolve in Different Directions?

Industrial policy can provide governments with knowledge, institutional networks, and organisational experience. However, repeated policy practice may strengthen certain capabilities while simultaneously weakening others.

Industrial policy can build and erode state capabilities at the same time.

The key is in understanding state capabilities in the plural. State capacity includes distinct capabilities, including appraisal, coordination, monitoring, verification, conditional implementation, and retention & reuse. This set of capabilities may complement one another, but tensions can also emerge under particular institutions. Industrial policy practice reshapes their composition and in turn influences what governments are capable of doing in following interventions.

2. Three Tensions in State Capability Formation

First, information accumulation and independent verification. Through sustained engagement with supported firms, public agencies may gradually acquire knowledge of production processes, supply chains, and market bottlenecks. However, increasing dependence on information supplied by policy beneficiaries may undermine independent technical cross-verification. ‘Knowing more’ and ‘being capable of making independent judgements’ are distinct organisational capabilities. This can be understood through Evans’s (1995) concept of embedded autonomy: effective embeddedness must be accompanied by organisational autonomy.

Second, coordination efficiency and conditional implementation. Long-term industrial cooperation may generate efficient interdepartmental procedures and stable trust between governments and firms. These relationships may also make it more difficult for governments to revise subsidy conditions, demand improvements in performance, or withdraw ineffective support. Amsden’s reciprocal control mechanisms and Pipkin’s (2026) discussion of the multiple forms of industrial discipline remind us that government support for firms must be accompanied by information, rules, and a credible capacity to act.

Third, organisational memory and adaptability. Successful industrial projects may establish clear administrative routines that reduce coordination costs in subsequent interventions. However, procedures that once proved effective may become entrenched evaluation frameworks, limiting an organisation’s capacity to recognise new technological trajectories. Nelson and Winter’s (1982) analysis of routines and March’s (1991) distinction between exploration and exploitation help illuminate this tension. Organisations must retain experience while preserving the ability to revise what they have learned.

3. How Can Political Choice Shape the Composition of State Capabilities?

Government learning is always embedded in the distribution of power and resources. Who holds the authority to interpret technical information? Who determines the beneficiaries of industrial policy? Who can enforce the withdrawal of policy support? These questions influence which forms of knowledge public organisations accumulate and how they employ them. Industrial coalitions, bureaucratic agencies, and public missions all shape this process of selection.

Which organisational capabilities are cultivated, which continue to receive institutional authority, and which are gradually marginalised through institutional evolution? The configuration of capabilities emerging from policy practice may expand the government’s scope for action, but it may also increase dependence on particular firms or administrative departments.

4. Returning to the Endogenous Formation of State Capacity

Industrial policy can build state capabilities through practice, but the direction and durability of capability accumulation are shaped by political and organisational arrangements. What ultimately deserves examination is the capability composition that governments develop through successive episodes of industrial transformation, and how that composition shapes the possibilities for future development.

State capacity is historically constituted. It can be built, exercised, preserved, and reorganised, while also being constrained by established policy relationships. Yes, the political economy of industrial policy should also be concerned with how the state itself develops.

Tao Xu, 许韬, 24 Nov 2026
