State Capacity for Industrial Policy Is an Organisational Learning Problem: Theoretical Connections

Research Note · 8 October 2026

Across Theoretical Traditions?

In discussions of industrial policy, ‘state capacity’ is often reduced to a question of whether government is sufficiently powerful. Yet industrialisation involves several distinct capabilities: understanding industrial structures, organising investment, monitoring firms’ performance, adjusting the conditions of policy support, and learning from past experience. Their origins, organisational carriers, and political bargains differ.

My theoretical research on Learning by Doing Industrial Policy brings together five interconnected intellectual traditions to explain the capabilities of practice: the capabilities that public organisations develop through actual policy work and can mobilise in subsequent interventions.

First, classical political economy and theories of late industrialisation explain why capabilities need to be cultivated. Hamilton and List examined the historical development of productive powers; Gerschenkron (1962) highlighted institutional substitution and historical variation in late industrialisation. This tradition situates capability formation within long-term structural transformation.

Second, Hirschman’s development economics explains what triggers capability formation. Through linkages and unbalanced growth, industrialisation generates new problems of interdependence that demand adjustments in decisions, investment, and organisational arrangements. The problems themselves create opportunities for learning.

Third, developmental-state scholarship asks who organises and disciplines learning. Amsden’s emphasis on industrial discipline and Evans’s concept of embedded autonomy both underscore information exchange between states and firms and the importance of conditional policy support.

Fourth, evolutionary economics and organisational theory explain how learning persists. Penrose, Nelson and Winter, Levitt and March, and Zollo and Winter connect experience, collective knowledge, routines, and memory, making learning an organisational process that can accumulate over time but may also become rigid.

Fifth, contemporary industrial policy research examines how capabilities evolve. Ang’s (2016) adaptive political economy, Andrews and colleagues’ Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA), Karo and Kattel’s (2018) evolutionary approach to policy capacity, Kattel and Mazzucato’s (2018) dynamic capabilities in the public sector, and Seidl and Wuttke’s (2026) research on strategic sector identification all return organisational learning to the concrete practice of policymaking.

From Abstract State Capacity to Six Concrete Capabilities

Drawing on these traditions, I provisionally distinguish six state organisational capabilities: appraisal (diagnosis and strategic judgement), coordination, monitoring, verification, conditional implementation, and adaptive retention and reuse.

This perspective depicts industrial policy as an evolving process of practice. Existing capabilities shape policy action; action reveals new problems; problems induce learning; public organisations retain, adapt, and apply what they have learned; and the resulting configuration of capabilities reshapes subsequent policy choices. The state is both an agent of industrial transformation and a learner within that transformation. Why do some capabilities accumulate while others stagnate or even erode through similar policy practices? This question directs the research towards the political economy of capability formation.
