An Uneven Ladder of China’s Political Elites: The Political Economy of Local Development and Cadre Allocation

Why Some Cities Made Political Careers, Some Not?

13 October 2026 Tao Louie Xu

In China, the political rewards of governing a city may rely as much on the city per se as on the cadre sent to govern it.

Shenzhen, Suzhou, and Hefei evoke industrial expansion, technological innovation, capital accumulation, and a seemingly inexhaustible succession of opportunities. For local officials still climbing the career ladder, these are exacting appointments, which may also serve as platforms for accumulating political capital and securing further advancement.

Tianjin and Chongqing—a different story?

In recent years, their municipal Party secretaries have generally also been members of the Party’s Politburo, signifying a higher formal political rank than that of their counterparts in ordinary prefecture-level or sub-provincial cities. Yet seniority need not confer comparable room for policy manoeuvre, readily attributable economic achievements, or prospects of further promotion. Extensive administrative responsibilities, inherited fiscal constraints, and intricate political co-ordination may produce a different configuration of career incentives.

An intriguing paradox emerges. A more senior political appointment may offer increasingly scarce opportunities for career advancement, whereas a larger economy may leave its government with limited discretionary resources relative to its responsibilities.

How much of an official’s political career reflects individual competence and governing performance? How much depends on their initial posting, the developmental opportunities attached to that position, and the organisational relationships they possess?

These questions lead to a broader examination of the political economy of cadre allocation. How do political careers, local development, central personnel decisions, and informal networks interact over time?

1. Political Generations: Younger Mayors and Party Secretaries, Senior Politburo Members

In October 2026, the Party secretaries of several economically dynamic Chinese cities belonged to a remarkably similar political generation. Although mayors and Party secretaries occupy distinct positions in China’s local political system, the following comparison focuses on Party secretaries and their career trajectories.

The five Party secretaries were born between 1969 and 1972, approximately a decade later than Tianjin’s M. Chen and Chongqing’s J. Yuan, who were 66 and 64 respectively.

A decade matters in a political career.

The younger cohort generally possesses substantial higher education and experience across administrative tiers, departments, and provinces. Their backgrounds encompass economic management, provincial Party organisation work, state-owned enterprise leadership, and central government administration.

These credentials reflect extensive professional experience and organisational selection, although they offer no direct measure of individual intelligence or governing competence. J. Yuan himself holds an engineering doctorate and previously managed major national aerospace programmes.

The more consequential distinction lies in their political career horizons.

Shenzhen, Chengdu, and Xiamen are sub-provincial cities. Suzhou and Hefei are prefecture-level cities whose Party secretaries may concurrently serve on provincial Party standing committees, thereby holding vice-provincial rank. Several of the five leaders had already attained such rank before their municipal appointments.

These positions offer opportunities to accumulate complex governing experience, demonstrate performance, and pursue further advancement within provincial and national leadership structures.

Politburo membership represents a different career stage. Officials have already entered the highest tier of political leadership below the Standing Committee, while the number of positions available for further advancement remains exceptionally limited.

The distinction resonates with career concerns theory: the expected returns to performance, remaining promotion opportunities, and perceived costs of failure evolve over an official’s professional lifetime.

Political rank, career prospects, and governing incentives may therefore follow different trajectories.

2. Two Municipalities, Divergent Political Careers

The personnel histories of Tianjin and Chongqing since 1997 reveal contrasting patterns.

Tianjin’s municipal leadership has consistently involved comparatively senior cadres. L. Zhang was approximately 58 when he became Party secretary in 1997; G. Zhang was 60 in 2007; C. Sun was 62 in 2012; H. Li was 60 in 2016; and M. Chen was 62 in 2022.

This concentration around the age of 60 suggests a durable pattern of mature-career appointments.

Their subsequent trajectories, however, diverged. G. Zhang entered the Politburo Standing Committee and subsequently became vice-premier. C. Sun moved to the United Front Work Department before becoming vice-premier in 2018. H. Li retained Politburo membership and became a vice-chairman of the National People’s Congress Standing Committee in 2023.

Tianjin has thus repeatedly accommodated senior political figures, including those approaching the later stages of their careers, while remaining a possible route to important central appointments.

Chongqing’s history is more turbulent.

Under G. He and Y. Wang, Chongqing served as a prominent platform for further political advancement. Y. Wang was only 50 when appointed in 2005, moving two years later to Guangdong before eventually entering the Politburo Standing Committee.

The municipality also accommodated other personnel functions. Z. Huang moved into the National People’s Congress system, while D. Zhang temporarily assumed the municipal leadership following X. Bo’s removal in 2012.

The subsequent decade marked a significant departure. The political careers of X. Bo and Z. Sun ended amid major cases. M. Chen, appointed in 2017 at the age of 56 and elevated to the Politburo later that year, remained a senior political figure but did not enter its Standing Committee in 2022. He subsequently moved laterally to Tianjin.

The latest appointment added another dimension. J. Yuan, previously Zhejiang’s Party secretary, assumed office in Chongqing in 2022 at the age of 60, bringing extensive experience in engineering management and provincial governance.

These trajectories suggest that Chongqing has performed successive, sometimes overlapping functions: a platform for promising political figures, a destination for experienced senior cadres, and a site of exceptional political reorganisation.

Has its promotional function weakened?

The contrast with Shanghai is particularly instructive. Between 2007 and 2022, three successive Shanghai Party secretaries, Z. Yu, Z. Han, and Q. Li, entered the Politburo Standing Committee in the political cycles in which they left office. Beijing’s Q. Cai likewise advanced to the Standing Committee in 2022.

Chongqing’s corresponding outcomes were markedly different.

This divergence in observed career mobility is striking. Nevertheless, the limited number of appointments, exceptional political cases, and individual circumstances complicate any inference about an enduring hierarchy of municipal posts.

A more intriguing question concerns the historical transformation of the positions themselves: could changes in Chongqing’s political function be connected to the evolution of its local development model?

3. When Growth Slows, Political Opportunities Change

A decade and a half ago, Chongqing was a city in extraordinary motion.

Infrastructure construction, industrial parks, foreign investment, automobile manufacturing, and rapid urbanisation transformed the municipality into a major centre of inland industrialisation.

Its real GDP expanded by 17.1 per cent in 2010 and 16.4 per cent in 2011. Tianjin experienced similarly extraordinary growth, reaching 17.4 per cent in 2010.

Such expansion provided abundant opportunities for visible achievements. New factories, industrial clusters, transport networks, and urban districts could materialise within a single political term.

Growth also conferred a narrative advantage. A mayor or Party secretary could associate industrial expansion and urban transformation with a coherent developmental programme, making achievements easier to identify, communicate, and incorporate into subsequent political evaluations.

Yet these achievements drew upon inherited industrial structures, accumulated investment, earlier institutional reforms, and support from higher levels of government.

By the late 2010s, Chongqing’s economic circumstances had changed substantially. GDP growth declined from 10.7 per cent in 2016 to 9.3 per cent in 2017 and 6.0 per cent in 2018, reflecting national structural adjustments, industrial cycles, and the diminishing momentum of investment-led expansion.

Political upheaval and economic deceleration unfolded within broadly the same historical period, although their coincidence alone cannot establish a direct causal relationship.

By 2025, Chongqing’s growth stood at 5.3 per cent, compared with Tianjin’s 4.8 per cent. Both municipalities had entered a period increasingly characterised by industrial upgrading, accumulated public obligations, fiscal adjustment, and risk management.

Their constraints, however, arose from different institutional geographies.

Tianjin lives in Beijing’s economic shadow.

The capital concentrates national research institutions, universities, central government agencies, corporate headquarters, and advanced producer services. These agglomeration economies offer opportunities for technological spillovers, while simultaneously attracting resources that might otherwise circulate through neighbouring cities.

Geographical proximity guarantees neither economic integration nor equal access to innovation.

In 2014, technology contracts exported from Beijing to Tianjin and Hebei accounted for only 4.8 per cent of the value exported to other provincial-level jurisdictions. This indicates limited regional technological diffusion at that time, although subsequent integration policies, industrial relocation, and the transfer of certain non-capital functions have also created new opportunities.

Tianjin’s economic trajectory is further shaped by its industrial and port infrastructure.

The catastrophic explosions at Tianjin Port in August 2015 exposed grave deficiencies in hazardous-materials management and regulatory oversight. The subsequent investigation and accountability process demonstrated the potentially severe political consequences of public-safety failures.

Such events may leave an enduring institutional memory, increasing the importance of regulatory compliance, emergency preparedness, and administrative accountability.

Chongqing inherited a different set of responsibilities.

Established as a centrally administered municipality in March 1997, it incorporated the former Chongqing municipality, Wanxian, Fuling, and Qianjiang. This administrative reorganisation reduced Sichuan’s governing burden, redistributed provincial jurisdiction, and facilitated Three Gorges resettlement and development along the upper Yangtze.

Covering approximately 82,400 square kilometres, Chongqing encompasses a major industrial metropolis alongside extensive mountainous, rural, and less-developed districts.

Its administrative boundaries consequently contain considerable economic and social heterogeneity.

In 2025, urban residents’ annual per-capita disposable income stood at approximately RMB 51,900, compared with RMB 23,400 for rural residents, a ratio of roughly 2.21.

These disparities entail substantial responsibilities for regional redistribution, public services, infrastructure, and long-term development.

Tianjin and Chongqing thus confront different manifestations of a shared challenge: how to cultivate new developmental opportunities while managing the economic structures and institutional commitments inherited from earlier periods.

4. Fiscal Constraints and the Asymmetry of Political Risk

The aggregate size of a local economy reveals relatively little about the discretionary resources available to its government.

A more informative measure concerns fiscal capacity relative to expenditure responsibilities.

Chongqing’s economy was approximately 1.8 times the size of Tianjin’s, yet its locally raised general public budget revenue was only 1.23 times as large.

Its annual expenditure approached RMB 570 billion, reflecting the demands of governing a much larger population across a considerably more extensive territory.

Central transfers remain indispensable. Chongqing received approximately RMB 242 billion in central general public budget subsidies in 2025, while Tianjin recorded around RMB 76 billion in net central transfers. Differences in fiscal arrangements prevent these figures from establishing a straightforward comparison of overall fiscal stress.

Moreover, statutory local-government debt excludes the wider liabilities of local-government financing vehicles. Debt sustainability depends on maturities, assets, repayment sources, and intergovernmental fiscal support.

Nevertheless, accumulated commitments can constrain the resources available for new policy initiatives.

Chongqing’s production of 1.296 million new-energy vehicles in 2025 also illustrates how substantial industrial dynamism can coexist with considerable fiscal responsibilities.

This distinction has political consequences.

During periods of rapid expansion, investment projects and industrial development can create conspicuous performance records. As growth matures, fiscal consolidation, infrastructure maintenance, public safety, and structural transformation demand greater attention. Their benefits may materialise gradually, often extending beyond a single official’s tenure.

Political accountability, meanwhile, remains immediate.

The promotion tournament framework connects observable local performance to political advancement. H. Li and L.-A. Zhou (2005) establish a positive association between provincial leaders’ economic performance and promotion in their 1979–1995 sample.

Yet this relationship varies across institutional settings and career stages.

Examining advancement within the CCP Central Committee, V. Shih, C. Adolph, and M. Liu (2012) report no robust positive association between economic growth and advancement in Party rank. Political connections, educational credentials, and fiscal revenues assume greater explanatory importance in their analysis.

For an official seeking entry into higher political office, industrial development and policy innovation may yield substantial career rewards. For a Politburo member approaching the later stages of a political career, further advancement is confined to a handful of positions.

The marginal career returns to additional economic achievements may therefore diminish, even as the potential political consequences of serious governance failures remain substantial.

This creates a possible asymmetry between career rewards and political accountability.

Mayors and Party secretaries overseeing regions with fiscal constraints, industrial adjustment, and complex public obligations may consequently attach greater importance to risk management, institutional stability, and policy co-ordination.

Such priorities may also represent prudent governance. Restraining inefficient investment and managing public debt can produce considerable long-term value, even when the benefits carry limited immediate political visibility.

A crucial distinction remains: the probability of serious governance failures and the career consequences attached to them are separate empirical questions. Historical political cases involving Tianjin and Chongqing illustrate the latter, but do not establish that serious failures occur more frequently in these municipalities than elsewhere.

5. Formal Rank, Informal Networks, and the Distribution of Political Power

Political authority cannot be inferred from formal rank alone.

Within the Politburo, members occupy the same formal political tier but exercise different institutional responsibilities.

Officials overseeing central organisation, discipline inspection, propaganda, or other nationwide functions possess administrative and organisational instruments distinct from those governing provincial-level jurisdictions.

Three dimensions are especially relevant:

Formal Political Rank: an official’s recognised position within the Party hierarchy.

Institutional Jurisdiction: authority over organisations, personnel, administrative resources, and policy domains.

Informal Political Influence: influence derived from organisational trust, historical working relationships, and political networks.

The municipal Party secretaries of Tianjin and Chongqing command substantial authority within their jurisdictions, although their direct responsibilities differ from those of Politburo members leading national institutions.

Actual political influence also depends on networks accumulated across successive appointments.

Public cadre biographies provide several observable indicators: superior–subordinate relationships, overlapping employment, shared institutional experience, and promotion histories.

For example, M. Chen served on Zhejiang’s provincial Party standing committee and headed its propaganda department during the period when J. Xi was provincial Party secretary. Their biographies establish an overlapping hierarchical working relationship.

Such connections may facilitate organisational trust, political communication, and policy co-ordination. Claims about private loyalty, factional membership, or the informal motives behind individual appointments require additional evidence.

Shared educational backgrounds, hometowns, central ministry experience, and careers in major state-owned enterprises also provide material for reconstructing elite networks.

Political–business relations constitute another dimension. State-owned enterprises, private manufacturers, local financing vehicles, and industrial investment institutions can provide governments with information, capital, and channels for policy implementation. These institutional relationships warrant careful distinction from improper personal financial interests.

The academic literature illuminates their broader political implications.

R. Jia, M. Kudamatsu, and D. Seim (2015) demonstrate that political connections and economic performance may complement one another in the selection of provincial leaders.

J. Jiang (2018) examines how patronage networks can strengthen bureaucratic co-ordination and economic performance through trust and incentive alignment.

R. Si and G. Xu (2024) further examine how promotion-based patronage networks may facilitate subsequent advancement, while exposing officials to career risks when associated senior leaders lose political standing.

Political connections may consequently influence both the resources available during an appointment and the durability of an official’s career prospects.

The effective political value of a position emerges from the interaction between formal authority, institutional jurisdiction, and the organisational networks of its occupant.

Political rank, observed career mobility, and actual decision-making influence may therefore diverge considerably.

6. The Endogenous Allocation of Political Talent and Performance Opportunities

A final question brings these arguments together.

If mayors and Party secretaries advance through successful local governance, who determines the developmental opportunities available to them in the first place?

Consider two similarly qualified cadres.

One assumes leadership of a prosperous industrial city with established manufacturing clusters, substantial fiscal revenues, dense business networks, and abundant investment opportunities.

The other is assigned to a region confronting debt pressures, industrial decline, extensive public expenditure commitments, and difficult structural reforms.

After several years, their economic performance may diverge substantially.

Yet these outcomes reflect interacting forces: individual leadership, inherited productive capabilities, institutional resources, upper-level policy support, and the initial assignment itself.

The question becomes more consequential if personnel authorities consider an official’s political potential when selecting their destination.

Promising cadres may be assigned to economically dynamic cities where opportunities for measurable achievements are plentiful. Those achievements subsequently strengthen their claims to further advancement.

This suggests a mechanism of endogenous allocation of political talent and performance opportunities.

Political career capital influences initial assignments; assignments determine the developmental opportunities and constraints officials encounter; these conditions shape governing performance; and observed performance, alongside political relationships and organisational needs, informs subsequent personnel decisions.

The process is dynamic and potentially self-reinforcing.

It can also operate in the opposite direction. Central and provincial authorities may assign experienced, politically trusted officials to struggling regions precisely because those environments demand complex problem-solving and risk management.

Two forms of placement may therefore coexist.

Promotion-oriented placement assigns cadres to positions offering political development, administrative experience, and opportunities for visible achievement.

Problem-solving placement directs officials towards difficult governance environments requiring institutional repair, organisational co-ordination, or structural transformation.

S. X. Chen, Y. Liu, and X. Xu (2020), drawing on prefecture-level personnel data from 1983 to 1998, identify political cycles in cadre appointments and an important role for previous working relationships. They also document higher subsequent reported GDP growth and investment-to-GDP ratios following appointments made under incumbent provincial Party secretaries.

Their analysis illustrates how personnel control, organisational relationships, and economic performance may become mutually connected.

The broader implication concerns selection bias.

If officials with favourable career prospects are systematically assigned to economically advantageous locations, conventional estimates linking governing performance to political promotion may conflate individual competence with opportunities embedded in initial appointments.

Likewise, officials managing difficult economies may achieve less spectacular growth despite undertaking demanding and consequential institutional reforms.

A convincing account of political advancement therefore requires attention to both who governs and where they are assigned to govern.

Local development itself can transform the opportunities attached to particular positions. A city once associated with rapid industrial expansion may subsequently become a site of fiscal consolidation, industrial upgrading, and administrative risk management.

These transformations may reshape the incentives attached to political office and influence the types of cadres selected to govern.

Tianjin and Chongqing offer valuable historical settings for investigating these processes. Their political trajectories, changing developmental opportunities, and extensive governance responsibilities reveal the limitations of interpreting political careers through formal rank or economic performance alone.

The deeper question concerns the distribution of political opportunity itself.

Does governing performance determine an official’s political opportunities, or does the initial allocation of political opportunities already shape the performance that the official can later claim?

Both mechanisms may operate simultaneously, reinforcing one another across successive appointments and political cycles.

On the uneven ladder of China’s political elites, how far a cadre climbs may depend on their ability to govern, the institutional resources they inherit, the political networks they navigate, and, crucially, the city to which they were sent.
