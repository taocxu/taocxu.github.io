Industrial Policy Comes Back, But Has Political Economy Changed?

Politics of Economics: Workshop to Honour Mario Pianta’s Work’| Scuola Normale Superiore | Tao Xu | 23 Nov 2026

Opening Reflections: Economics as Political Practice

The workshop opened with reflections on Mario Pianta’s intellectual and institutional contributions. From the perspective of economic sociology, Guglielmo Meardi offered an amusing provocation, i.e. economists’ models can be unrealistic and, through their performativity, can bring into existence the very economic behaviour they purport to describe. The discussion then turned to Pianta as a reminder of the diversity within economics. Across his work on industry, labour, war and peace, Pianta has connected economic outcomes to underlying structures, interests, and relations of power. Alberto Quadrio Curzio complemented this portrait by recalling Pianta’s interdisciplinary work and contributions to Italian academic and policy institutions, highlighting his pluralism, intellectual rigour and commitment to long-term solidaristic development.

Francesco Saraceno: Crises and Persistence of Macroeconomic Paradigms

In A Missed Opportunity? The Crises and Paradigm Competition in Economic Policy, Francesco Saraceno traced the trajectory from Friedman’s monetarism to the New Keynesian Consensus and its influence on the institutional design of European Economic and Monetary Union. The 2008 financial crisis appeared to reopen questions that the prevailing consensus had largely marginalised. Discretionary fiscal policy, public investment, the limits of market self-correction, and the relationship between demand and long-run growth. Yet Europe’s response to the sovereign debt crisis remained strongly shaped by the earlier commitment to fiscal discipline and market adjustment. The subsequent debate over secular stagnation briefly restored attention to persistently weak demand and falling equilibrium interest rates; post-pandemic inflation then returned monetary tightening to the foreground. His overarching question was whether repeated crises had genuinely transformed macroeconomic thinking, or merely produced temporary departures from an enduring policy paradigm.

Antonio Andreoni: ‘Industrial Policy Back… Really?’

Antonio Andreoni’s presentation supplied the morning’s most impressive critique of industrial policy’s apparent revival. Since the global financial crisis, industrial policy has returned to public debate in response to economic stagnation, climate change, the pandemic, industrial competition, and geopolitical rivalry. Its vocabulary has become legitimate again. The policy once seen as ‘the policy that shall not be named’ can be discussed openly at this stage. Yet Andreoni separated the normalisation of a policy label from a transformation in economic theory, public institutions, and productive structures.

Antonio’s opening questions were particularly pointed. Have evolutionary, structuralist, and institutional political economists prevailed in the long-standing debate over industrial policy? Have their ideas actually informed European policymaking? And is the mainstreaming of industrial policy delivering the changes required across economies, societies, and ecologies? He organised the historical discussion around five overlapping moments, that is, the global financial crisis, the Paris Agreement, Made in China 2025, COVID-19, and the intensification of trade and geopolitical conflict. The following critiques capture the argument as I understood it from the presentation.

1. Europe’s Market-centred Orthodoxy: Industrial Policy without State Capacity

Andreoni began with an institutional criticism, describing the EU as having ‘adhered religiously to the neoclassical doctrine’ since the 1980s, thereby losing ‘state capacity in designing and implementing’ industrial policy while narrowing its policy space. The single market and competition rules remained the organising principles of European growth policy. Even when industrial policy returned after 2008, the response was, in his words that is very accurate I believe, ‘a timid return’.

He located these limitations in identifiable instruments. Smart Specialisation tended towards horizontal, deliberately non-strategic support and attention to small and medium-sized enterprises, despite the importance of large anchor firms for industrial ecosystems (in China we call them the lead firms 领军企业). Horizon 2020 offered research funding without sufficient strategic technological direction. Structural funds frequently compensated regions for the costs of adjustment rather than creating new production capacities. He likewise questioned a European Investment Bank approach oriented towards financial returns and de-risking, with insufficient effort on creating markets and industries. His comparison was with the United States, which had continued to practise selective industrial intervention, sometimes under the rubric of national security.

Italy was, for Andreoni, the especially revealing case and for me one of the most rewarding cases, described through the expression ‘more Catholic than the Pope’. At a time of industrial contraction, the language of expansionary austerity coexisted with calls to reduce state aid. Meanwhile, loan guarantees, machinery incentives, R&D tax credits, and tax reductions remained fragmented, very fragmented when I a Chinese try to compare it with China. The public investment institution Cassa Depositi e Prestiti (CDP) had not fully assumed the role of an ‘industrial orchestrator’. The underlying criticism concerned the absence of a coordinated developmental strategy connecting finance, technological capabilities, productive investment, and macroeconomic conditions. A collection of incentives does not, by itself, amount to industrial transformation.

2. The European Green Deal: Environmental Ambition without a Transformative Industrial Strategy

The second criticism addressed the European Green Deal, drawing explicitly on work by Mario Pianta and Matteo Lucchese. Andreoni acknowledged the political significance of bringing decarbonisation into the centre of European policy. Nevertheless, he identified a ‘lack of strategic vision’ for a just post-carbon economy, adding that the ‘government [was] still in the back seat’.

His argument turned on the distinction between correcting market failures and organising structural change. Carbon pricing and competitive auctions can alter investment incentives, but they do not necessarily coordinate the complementary investments required for industrial decarbonisation. Steel, for example, depends on technological change alongside energy infrastructure, finance, equipment, workforce skills and reliable demand. Andreoni criticised continued reliance on market mechanisms, the absence of a ‘coherent macroeconomic framework and integration with industrial policy’, inadequate resources, and limited implementation capabilities.

The territorial dimension was equally important. He argued that, despite the Just Transition Mechanism, the Green Deal had not been sufficiently conceived as an opportunity to restructure industrial specialisation across Europe. Who produces the technologies of transition? Which regions accumulate capabilities, and which become dependent on imports? Could Southern European economies acquire stronger positions in emerging energy and manufacturing systems? These are about the geography of productive development and distributive outcomes, not simply the aggregate volume of emissions reduced.

3. NextGenerationEU: Large-scale Finance, Weak Strategic Coordination and Unequal Capabilities

Andreoni treated the pandemic response as another opening for industrial transformation. NextGenerationEU and the Recovery and Resilience Facility mobilised substantial public resources, yet his assessment emphasised ‘loose conditionalities and coordination’, summarised on the slide as ‘more of the same, too little of the new’.

He identified two related problems. First, strategic heterogeneity: member states used common European financing for markedly different purposes. Some investments addressed immediate or inherited shortcomings; others strengthened longer-term productive capacity. Andreoni contrasted the industrial and energy trajectories of countries such as Italy and Spain to illustrate why spending totals alone reveal little about the quality or direction of transformation. Second, compounding effects: countries already possessing stronger fiscal resources, administrative capacity and industrial systems, notably Germany and France, could exploit more fully the relaxation of state-aid constraints. A common European instrument might therefore coexist with, or even amplify, uneven national capacities.

The criticism requires an important distinction. RRF disbursements are subject to formal milestones and targets. Andreoni’s concern was the strategic content and coordination of the investments, rather than an absence of legal conditions. His broader question was whether European financing would reorganise industrial capabilities and reduce structural asymmetries, instead of leaving each national economy largely to reproduce its existing position.

4. US–China Industrial Race: Europe’s Reactive Strategy and Limits of Technocratic ‘De-risking’

The fourth critique situated European policy in a changing international political economy. Andreoni described the US response to China’s industrial ascent through successive stages of protectionism, Biden’s Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), and this time techno-nationalism. He characterised the EU’s response as a ‘slow realisation of the competitiveness challenge’ and a ‘reactive mode’, illustrating the limits through cases including Kuka and the proposed Alstom–Siemens merger.

The theoretical tension was between short-term efficiency gains and dynamic efficiency. Existing competition and state-aid frameworks prioritise current prices and allocative efficiency while undervaluing learning, cumulative capabilities, technological upgrading, and the coordination of investment. Andreoni’s argument challenged the adequacy of evaluating productive transformation solely through a short-term market-efficiency aspect.

He then considered strategic autonomy and the Draghi Report through an unusually clear three-part answer. Yes: Europe increasingly acknowledges the necessity of sectoral and technological targeting. No: ‘the state is back mainly as a de-risking, regulatory and technocratic player more than a political player driving transformation’. No again: macroeconomic, industrial and competition policy measures are not strategically coordinated to create a virtuous circle of investment, technological progress, and employment.

This also explained his objection to industrial policy being reduced to a ‘technical exercise’. Structural change redistributes profits, jobs, bargaining power, and productive opportunities. It demands conflict management and the deliberate reconfiguration of interests. A further warning concerned the global fallacy of composition. Strategies that appear reasonable for each country pursuing technological sovereignty may, when pursued simultaneously, intensify subsidy competition, economic fragmentation, and conflict. Industrial policy must consequently address international economic relations and global commons, as well as national competitiveness.

5. Theoretical ‘Epicycles’: Fitting Industrial Policy into Neoclassical Economics without Reconsidering Premises

Andreoni’s final criticism concerned the economics profession itself. Employing their work as Chang, Andreoni and Silva (2026), he asked whether industrial policy had genuinely returned in economic theory. His analogy was to the epicycles of Ptolemaic astronomy. Additional devices introduced to reconcile observations with an inherited explanatory system.

He described ‘theoretical retrofitting and new neoclassical justifications for industrial policy’. The target was a strand of mainstream scholarship that selectively incorporates successful industrial-policy episodes into a market-failure framework without sufficiently, for me if not any, revisiting the historical and institutional foundations of industrial development. He cited South Korea and criticised the treatment of particular policy episodes as an ‘econometric playground’ detached from their political and economic setting, that is historical.

The concluding sentence was especially forceful: ‘These “re-drawing” exercises are not neutral, as they in fact re-write history to confirm and reinforce the neoclassical paradigm.’ The point concerns explanatory authority which historical facts are selected, which causal mechanisms are admitted, and whether the state is treated as a temporary corrector of market imperfections or as an institution capable of shaping production systems, learning and structural interdependencies. This need not invalidate rigorous econometric work. It does demand that causal estimates be situated within the institutional arrangements, histories of capability formation and political conflicts that made the observed policy possible in the first place.

Beyond the Critique: Industrial Policy with Transformative Purpose

Andreoni closed with a constructive outline for ‘industrial policy with a transformative purpose and a new centrality in economic policy’. Industrial policy should:

  1. Direct technological change;
  2. Align technological, social and environmental objectives;
  3. Connect investment with technological development and employment;
  4. Widen opportunities for social mobility and distributed productive capacity;
  5. Contribute to a reconstituted social contract.

It should also safeguard the global commons, including the climate, ecosystems and biodiversity.

For these aims to become actionable, he called for strategic coordination, alignment and synchronisation across policy domains; conditionality and reciprocal commitments in public–private and public–public relationships; the active governing of competition; an appropriate macroeconomic framework; and the rebuilding of capabilities through ‘learning by doing industrial policy’ (see my notes on this: How Industrial Policy Builds State Capacity; Learning by Doing Industrial Policy; State Capacity for Industrial Policy). The distinction running throughout was between the proliferation of industrial-policy measures and the institutional capacity to direct and sustain structural transformation.

My principal takeaway from the morning is a question of political economy and research design alike. What would count as evidence that the return of industrial policy has changed the direction of technological development, the distribution of productive capabilities, and the capacity of institutions to govern economic transformation? Policy announcements and expenditure are observable. The more demanding test is in the structures and outcomes they leave behind.

Tao Louie Xu, 许韬, 23 Nov 2026
