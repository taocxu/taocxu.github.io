BLOG & RESEARCH NOTES

Ihsaan Bassier on Land Dispossession, Surplus Labour, and Structural Transformation

7 October 2026 Tao Louie Xu

On 7 October 2026, I attended the first seminar of this year’s SOAS Economics Seminar Series. Ihsaan Bassier presented joint research with Josh Budlender and Surbhi Kesar on a persistent puzzle in development economics: if urban firms cannot fully absorb available labour, why do people continue to enter non-agricultural labour markets even when employment opportunities remain scarce?

Their answer redirects attention towards the supply side of structural transformation. Labour does not always leave agriculture because better jobs await elsewhere. Sometimes people enter wage labour markets because historical dispossession has deprived them of viable alternatives.

1. From Labour Absorption to Labour Supply

Conventional accounts of persistent surplus labour often begin with insufficient demand for workers: premature deindustrialisation, skills mismatches, spatial frictions, and the limited expansion of productive employment. Bassier and his co-authors ask a complementary question: why is labour supplied to the non-agricultural economy when firms cannot absorb it?

Land provides more than an agricultural asset. It secures food, subsistence income, and a fallback livelihood, offering households an outside option beyond the wage labour market. Land dispossession weakens that option. People may then migrate and seek wages even when the probability of finding employment remains low.

Land dispossession → weaker subsistence options → migration and wage job search → wage employment for some, unemployment or surplus labour for others.

The distinction matters. A movement out of agriculture need not correspond to an equivalent expansion of productive employment.

2. Apartheid, Forced Removals, and Livelihoods

South Africa provides a historically revealing case. Under apartheid, forced removals displaced Black communities from farms and other settlements, often into the so-called homelands. Records compiled by the Surplus People Project estimate that more than 3.5 million people were forcibly removed between 1960 and 1983; around 2 million were displaced from rural areas.

These removals served racial segregation and territorial control, but they also altered access to land and the conditions under which people could secure their livelihoods. Migration into urban labour markets remained restricted for much of the apartheid period. As mobility restrictions eased, especially around 1986, those with diminished subsistence opportunities faced stronger pressures to search for wage employment.

South Africa is also unusual for its exceptionally small share of self-employed agricultural workers. Compared with economies where smallholder agriculture or informal self-employment provides a livelihood when wage jobs are unavailable, South Africa offers a particularly stark setting in which to examine the consequences of losing that fallback.

3. Evidence: More Migration, Employment, and Surplus Labour

The research combines historical Surplus People Project records with individual migration histories and labour-market outcomes. The authors distinguish the dates of forced removals from subsequent migration, compare relocation sites with other homeland districts, and examine differences in the intensity of displacement.

The event-study evidence shows relatively stable migration patterns before documented removals, followed by an increase around and after the displacement events. Their empirical analysis, including reduced-form and instrumental-variable approaches, associates forced relocation with later migration and changes in labour allocation. The effects persist for 10–20 years after removal.

Crucially, relocation-induced migration is associated with an increase in wage employment and non-agricultural surplus labour. Some people obtain jobs; others enter labour markets that cannot fully accommodate them. These outcomes are not mutually exclusive at the aggregate level.

One measurement distinction is worth retaining. Increased market income after migration would not necessarily imply improved overall welfare, since subsistence production, access to land, and the security of a livelihood outside the wage economy may have been lost.

4. A Wider Account of Structural Transformation

The authors extend the argument through a modified Harris–Todaro model, in which migration incentives reflect not only expected urban wages and unemployment risk, but also the strength of rural subsistence options. A weaker agricultural outside option can induce migration even without stronger urban labour demand.

They also assemble land-inequality evidence from agricultural censuses covering more than 100 countries. Greater land inequality is associated with a smaller share of self-employed agricultural workers and more non-agricultural surplus labour. The cross-country patterns are consistent with the mechanism identified in South Africa, although these associations should not, by themselves, be treated as definitive causal evidence across countries.

The political-economy implication reaches beyond a conventional story of workers moving from low-productivity agriculture into higher-productivity industry. Dispossession can create people who must seek wages without creating sufficient jobs for them. This connects the paper to classical debates on surplus labour, including Lewis, and to later accounts of dispossession without full proletarianisation.

5. Institutions, Labour Allocation, and Development

The research offers an incisive connection between historical institutions and present-day labour allocation. Forced removal was not simply an event in the past. By undermining land-based subsistence, it altered the opportunities and constraints faced by households decades later.

It also complicates how we evaluate structural transformation. More wage employment, more migration, and higher measured market income do not necessarily constitute an unambiguous improvement when unemployment, livelihood insecurity, and the loss of subsistence options persist.

This engagement with substantive questions in development economics and political economy is especially relevant. The enduring influence of historical institutions on contemporary labour markets, and on the processes and practices of industrial policy, is also central to my research interests.
