Merve Sancak on Industrial Policy Beyond Hegemons: Policy Misalignment and Turkish Garment Industry

25 November 2026 | Tao Louie Xu | SOAS University of London

Why an industry struggles to upgrade, or not, when productive capabilities have been accumulated, global value chains have been engaged, and opportunities for further development have emerged from a changing international economic order? Why, under seemingly favourable conditions, might an established industry even begin to decline?

On 25 November 2026, Merve Sancak, a political economist at Loughborough University London, uncovered this development puzzle at the SOAS Global Development Seminar Series, with her recent research into Turkish garment industry. The seminar was chaired by Lorena Lombardozzi, with Ezgi Unsal from our Department of Economics and Kerem Coban from the School of Finance and Management as discussants.

The evening began with a little institutional amusement. At five o’clock, as the seminar was about to commence, another group arrived to claim the room for what appeared to be a six o’clock meeting. I have no idea. We had to relocate downstairs, where our discussion of industrial policy eventually unfolded. Welcome to SOAS!

1. Beyond Hegemons: Bridging Comparative and International Political Economy

Much of the contemporary debate on industrial policy revolves around geoeconomic competition between China, the United States, and the European Union. How is industrial policy formulated and practised in those beyond the major powers? In a recent special issue of the Journal of Economic Policy Reform, Merve Sancak and colleagues (2026) advance an analytical category: non-hegemonic powers (NHPs). Although those lack the dominant position from which to dictate their policy priority globally, they retain political agency and may exploit their productive capabilities, geopolitical positions, and regional economic relations to negotiate policy space for industrial development.

Two intellectual traditions inform this enquiry. Comparative political economy (CPE) examines domestic institutions, state capacity, state autonomy, and political interests in industrial policy. The developmental state literature, for instance, explains East Asian industrialisation through historically constituted institutions and state-business relations. International political economy (IPE), meanwhile, foregrounds global economic hierarchies, the power of transnational corporations, and the governance of global value chains (GVCs). Integration into global production may facilitate industrial learning, yet the capacity of domestic suppliers to capture value remains conditioned by their position vis-à-vis global lead firms.

Each tradition illuminates a dimension that the other tends to understate. Domestic institutions are situated within an international hierarchy of economic power; global structural constraints are mediated through domestic institutions and political agency. By bridging CPE and IPE, the special issue aims to understand industrial policy practised amidst the interplay of structural dependency and political agency.

2. The Turkish Puzzle: Productive Capabilities, Emerging Opportunities, but Industrial Decline

The Turkish garment industry (TGI) has been integrated into European and global garment chains for decades. Since the 1990s, European economic integration, the customs union with the EU and geographical proximity to European markets facilitated the development of a relatively comprehensive textile and garment production system. Domestic firms have accumulated capabilities in design, material sourcing, and full-package production. With the expansion of fast fashion, Turkish suppliers acquired a particular competitive advantage through their ability to respond to changing consumer demand and undertake comparatively sophisticated production activities.

The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent geoeconomic developments opened another window of opportunity. Several trajectories of upgrading were envisaged:

  • Product Upgrading: moving towards more sophisticated products, including medical protective equipment, military garments, and technical textiles.
  • Process Upgrading: investing in automation and advanced production technologies to raise efficiency and exploit opportunities arising from nearshoring.
  • Environmental Upgrading: responding to European sustainability requirements through investments in cleaner production, renewable energy and recycling infrastructure.
  • Branding: expanding Turkish brands into markets where established international competitors had withdrawn, notably Russia.

Opportunities were accompanied by various sectoral industrial policy instruments. Technological investment was supported through public credit provision; design and engineering capabilities were cultivated through training and education; incentives were introduced to facilitate geographical relocation, branding and the green transition. An established industrial base, accumulated productive capabilities, emerging market opportunities and supportive policy instruments appeared to converge.

Yet since 2023, the industry has struggled to sustain its competitiveness. Numerous firms have reduced production or ceased operating altogether. According to the figures presented by Merve, approximately 80,000 jobs were lost in 2024 alone.

What went wrong?

3. Industrial Policy in Turmoil: Macroeconomy, Labour, and Energy

Sancak (2026) attributes much of this predicament to the misalignment of sectoral industrial policy with broader economic policy. Industrial development unfolds within an economic system where access to finance, labour markets, energy provision, and international competitiveness are interdependent. Policy measures formulated to support a particular sector may consequently be undone by developments in other policy domains.

Macroeconomic Policy: Investment Capacity and Price Competitiveness

Against a backdrop of global financial tightening, Türkiye underwent successive shifts in monetary and exchange-rate policy, followed by a return towards a more orthodox macroeconomic orientation in 2023. Financial instability constrained firms’ access to credit. Meanwhile, domestic prices increased faster than the exchange rate adjusted. Firms struggled to finance technological investments necessary for upgrading, whilst their existing production activities became increasingly expensive relative to international competitors. Although targeted credit programmes had been introduced to facilitate investment, access remained limited for many smaller firms. The efficacy of sectoral industrial policy was consequently eroded by the broader macroeconomic environment.

Labour Policy: Production Costs and Competing Social Objectives

Garment producers sought to contain labour costs, with some industrial associations advocating regionally differentiated minimum wages. The government, however, maintained a nationally determined minimum wage that was raised considerably amid inflationary pressures. The expansion of retail and service activities also intensified competition for labour, rendering recruitment and retention increasingly difficult for garment manufacturers.

Energy Policy: Industrial Interests and Green Transition

The geopolitical turmoil surrounding the Russia–Ukraine war and rising international energy prices further increased production costs. Merve suggested that the interests of energy companies had been prioritised in aspects of Türkiye’s energy policy, whilst small and medium-sized garment producers were exposed to considerable cost pressures.

An interesting explanation emerged from interviews. Following the rise in natural gas prices, some Turkish producers sought to substitute coal for gas. Such adjustments were reportedly constrained by the environmental requirements imposed by European buyers, even whilst certain European producers were permitted to use coal. The example illustrates energy policy, environmental standards and unequal bargaining relations interact to structure the distribution of adjustment costs across borders.

4. Upgrading in Global Value Chains: Who Captures Value and Who Bears the Costs?

Environmental upgrading has increasingly been demanded by European buyers, yet the investment necessary to meet the requirements is often expected to be financed by suppliers. The costs of the green transition are thereby transferred down the value chain, whilst price remains a decisive consideration in sourcing decisions. During the pandemic, Türkiye’s proximity to European markets acquired renewed significance. As international transportation recovered, however, orders were redirected towards lower-cost producers in Asia and North Africa.

Geoeconomic competition and supply-chain reconfiguration may generate opportunities for industrial development. Whether opportunities can be realised relies on the investment capacity of domestic firms, the alignment of national economic policy, and the distribution of bargaining power along global value chains.

This invites a further reflection on what is meant by upgrading. The acquisition of design capabilities, the adoption of advanced machinery, and the production of more sophisticated goods all represent technological achievements. Whether such achievements translate into higher profitability and stronger bargaining power remains a question. In Sancak’s research, upgrading is therefore understood in relation to value capture by domestic suppliers.

How are accumulated productive capabilities translated into economic gains? How are the gains distributed among firms, workers, and the wider economy?

5. Discussants: Structural Interdependencies, Policy Coordination, and Policy Demand

Ezgi B. Unsal: Textiles, Garments, and Structural Transformation

Ezgi began with the distinction between textiles and garments, two industries that are often examined together. Whilst vertically interconnected, they are characterised by different production structures. Textile manufacturing generally involves larger-scale production, more substantial capital investments, and greater technological intensity, whereas garment production remains comparatively labour-intensive.

Their trajectories of upgrading consequently diverge. Technological progress and increased profitability among upstream textile producers can coexist with declining competitiveness and employment among downstream garment manufacturers. During the pandemic, for instance, Turkish textile producers found opportunities to export their products, potentially at the expense of domestic garment producers who depended on their supplies.

This reminded me of the structural interdependencies elaborated by Antonio Andreoni and Ha-Joon Chang (2019). An industry is constituted through its relations with other productive activities. Changes in one set of the production structure may facilitate or constrain development elsewhere. Analytically, the distinction between textiles and garments enables us to investigate that upgrading in one sector may generate tensions and bottlenecks in another.

Ezgi also raised an intriguing question concerning structural transformation. Under macroeconomic instability, when investments in capital-intensive industries become less attractive, one might expect established labour-intensive activities to regain prominence. The contraction of the Turkish garment industry complicates this expectation. Had a more coherent industrial strategy been pursued, should traditional garment production have been sustained, or should resources have been deliberately redirected towards activities with higher value added? The answer depends, among other things, on what we understand industrial development to achieve.

Kerem Coban: The Supply and Demand Sides of Industrial Policy

Kerem approached the research from a public policy perspective, foregrounding the design and coordination of policy mixes. Were policy objectives coherently articulated? Were the instruments deployed by different government agencies mutually consistent? These questions elucidate the institutional conditions under which industrial policy is formulated and implemented.

Of particular interest was the distinction between the supply and demand sides of industrial policy. Can the state supply the institutional support necessary for industrial upgrading? Do domestic entrepreneurs possess the capabilities, aspirations, and strategic orientations to demand and utilise such support? Some firms may advocate lower interest rates, currency depreciation, and cheaper labour whilst displaying limited capacity or commitment to undertake technological upgrading.

6. Jing’s Q&A: How Does Explanatory Framework Emerge?

Among the questions raised from the audience, I found Jing’s particularly instructive. Her questions concerned three aspects of the research: why industrial upgrading was framed primarily through state policy rather than firm capabilities; why macroeconomic policy, labour policy, and energy policy were selected as the principal explanatory dimensions; and how geoeconomic developments might reshape bargaining relations between global buyers, domestic firms, and the state.

The second question, in particular, caught my attention. An exchange along the following lines:

Jing: Why you selected these three policy domains? They emerge from your interviews with firms?

Merve: Yes… They came up during the interviews.

Paraphrased from the seminar discussion.

The significance of this exchange resides in the way an explanatory framework was developed through empirical enquiry. Merve’s research employs interviews with industrial agents and representatives of relevant organisations, alongside extensive documentary materials and data. The three policy domains through which the development puzzle was explained were identified during the investigation itself.

A Research Methodology Tip

We may enter the field with a clearly articulated research puzzle and theoretically informed questions, whilst remaining open to the explanatory categories that emerge from empirical investigation. Interviews and archival research may bring to light processes and relations that were scarcely considered when the research was initially designed. They encourage us to revisit the literature, refine our analytical categories, and reconsider the ways the phenomenon ought to be explained.

Research Puzzle → Empirical Investigation → Emergent Categories → Theoretical Engagement → Explanatory Framework

This illustrates an inductive dimension of empirical research and is a practical insight into abductive reasoning. Although the process has been rendered sequentially here, theoretical engagement and empirical investigation may repeatedly inform one another throughout the research.

For those of us undertaking historical, institutional, and political economy research, the methodological lesson is particularly valuable. Our theoretical knowledge enables us to recognise meaningful phenomena and propose questions. Yet we ought to grant the empirical world sufficient latitude to challenge our initial understandings, disclose unanticipated relations, and redirect the theoretical enquiry.

Enter the field with theoretically informed questions, and remain open to the explanatory categories and mechanisms that emerge from empirical investigation.

7. Rethinking Industrial Policy and Development

The seminar brought me back to three dimensions of the political economy of industrial policy articulated by Andreoni and Chang (2019): structural interdependencies, policy alignment, and conflict management. Industrial development happens within a historically constituted production structure. Financial institutions, labour markets, energy provision, technological capabilities, and global production relations co-condition the possibilities available to domestic firms. The policy measures through which these domains are governed are themselves embedded in political and institutional relations.

Policy misalignment can accordingly be seen as a phenomenon requiring further explanation. Different government agencies, industrial organisations, and social groups pursue objectives that are difficult to reconcile. How are the objectives constituted? How particular conceptions of development and policy priority come to be produced and reproduced through institutional and organisational practices? Under what conditions can conflicting interests and understandings be reconciled in the formulation and implementation of industrial policy? Resonating closely with my doctoral research into the institutional and ideological dimensions of industrial policy.

There remains a more fundamental issue concerning the purpose of upgrading. Towards the end of the discussion, Merve spoke of women who had lost their jobs following the closure of garment factories. Some may remain unemployed, withdraw from the labour market or return to agricultural activities characterised by less favourable working conditions. Their experiences render visible a dimension of structural transformation that can easily disappear from aggregate measures of industrial performance.

When capital-intensive activities advance technologically and particular firms capture greater value, whilst numerous workers lose their livelihoods, how should we evaluate the developmental significance of such transformation? Productive capabilities, employment, and the distribution of economic gains deserve to be examined together.

Industrial upgrading for whom, and at whose cost? For development economists, this remains as open as ever.
