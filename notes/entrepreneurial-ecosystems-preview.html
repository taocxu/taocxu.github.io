BLOG & RESEARCH NOTES

Entrepreneurial Ecosystems

12 December 2025 Tao Louie Xu

An entrepreneurial ecosystem (EE) is a system composed of interdependent agents, institutions, and contextual conditions. Ecological interactions among the elements of the system provide the foundation for identifying and exploiting entrepreneurial opportunities and foster productive entrepreneurship at a specific spatio-territorial scale (Stam, 2015; Malecki, 2018; Spigel & Harrison, 2018; Miles & Morrison, 2020; Audretsch & Belitski, 2021; Lange & Schmidt, 2021).

First, this definition understands entrepreneurship as an embedded process (Shane & Venkataraman, 2000; Stam, 2015; Spigel & Harrison, 2018), thereby relocating interpretations of the entrepreneurial process within relational and institutional environments. The identification and exploitation of entrepreneurial opportunities occur at concrete scales, including local and regional scales and, at times, the national dimension (Miles & Morrison, 2020; Audretsch & Belitski, 2021). The EE framework therefore extends and incorporates theories and ideas operating across territorial scales, including industrial districts, clusters, and innovation systems (Marshall, 1890; Krugman, 1990, 1991; Nelson, 1993; Freeman, 1995; Lundvall, 2010; Xu & Hu, 2024). It brings socio-institutional and organisational mechanisms into a framework centred on the entrepreneur and entrepreneurial opportunity (Autio et al., 2014; Stam, 2015; Malecki, 2018; Lange & Schmidt, 2021).

Second, the definition emphasises an ecological perspective. Entrepreneurial outcomes depend on co-evolutionary relations among heterogeneous agents and environmental conditions, moving beyond earlier theories’ emphasis on isolated firm resources or atomised individuals in an abstract market (Moore, 1993; Spilling, 1996). An ecosystem therefore denotes a continuously evolving assemblage comprising agents, activities and their outputs, processes, services, and diverse resources and productive capabilities. These elements enable the assemblage to be reproduced, reproduced anew, or mobilised within system-specific institutions and relations of complementarity and substitution.

When competition and value creation are understood as interactions within a business ecology, this ecological intuition becomes clearer: organisational agents form interdependent symbiotic relations while also imposing selective pressures on one another (Moore, 1993; Iansiti & Levien, 2004; Adner & Kapoor, 2010, 2016; Adner, 2017). A range of systemic structural factors shapes the business and innovation performance of a population of organisational agents, or agent organisations (Granstrand & Holgersson, 2020; Andreoni & Kattel, 2023; Andreoni & Anzolin, 2025).

A system depends on their dynamic connections, both across organisations and institutions and within them (Freeman, 1995). The success of an innovative firm often depends on the efforts of external innovators, while the external environment forms a structure of interdependencies. Innovation also depends on an agent’s position in the ecosystem relative to the focal firm. At this point, the system’s structural vertical integration has important consequences for overall outcomes (Adner & Kapoor, 2010, 2016; Adner, 2017). Inquiry must therefore specify the particular participants and institutions on which innovation-driven growth depends (Mazzucato, 2018).

EE research translates the ecological metaphor into a ‘systems ontology’. By emphasising coordination and complementarity among conditions, it explains variation in productive entrepreneurship across different spatio-territorial scales. Its central claim is that entrepreneurial outcomes rest on interdependencies. The identification and exploitation of entrepreneurial opportunities draw on agency, enabling institutional arrangements in which agency can operate, organisational supports, and relational infrastructures that reduce search, coordination, and experimentation costs.

Spilling (1996) systematically explicated the entrepreneurial system and explained why institutional and relational architectures are necessary for entrepreneurial opportunities to be converted into action (Shane & Venkataraman, 2000). Subsequent research has increasingly centred explanation on bundles of conditions, examining how different conditions jointly promote or impede productive entrepreneurship and why similar policy inputs generate divergent territorial outcomes (Stam, 2015; Audretsch & Belitski, 2017, 2021; Malecki, 2018). The significance of the EE approach depends on two requirements. First, productive entrepreneurship must be clearly defined. Second, the explanatory force of spatio-territorial conditions through coordination and complementarity must be specified (Stam, 2015). Within this framework, context is understood as a system of conditions that carries mechanisms, and its complementarities require theorisation and measurement.

Process-theorising consequently makes the EE approach more applicable to organisation-level research by shifting attention from endowments towards changes in their reproduction across space and territory. Coordination and complementarity are dynamic, and their effects depend on sequencing, feedback, and cumulative work that stabilises or reconfigures the entrepreneurial environment. Spigel and Harrison (2018) argue that ecosystems evolve through configurations in which cultural meanings, social relations, and material infrastructures co-evolve over time, generating path dependence as well as punctuated shifts.

Entrepreneurial ecosystems should therefore be treated as dynamic systems, directing inquiry towards adaptive processes of reproduction and, in some circumstances, the organisational work that regenerates entrepreneurial conditions (Stam, 2015; Auerswald & Dani, 2017; Spigel & Harrison, 2018). Given the heterogeneity of agents, their interactions, and evolutionary processes, effective ecosystems may take multiple viable configurations. Systematic complementarity means that different bundles of conditions may produce similar performance; the task is to identify viable combinations and pathways associated with high performance (Schrijvers et al., 2024).

Lange and Schmidt (2021) accordingly position EEs as a bridging concept connecting entrepreneurship to regional development and regional value creation (Audretsch & Belitski, 2021). This position avoids reducing explanation to innovation-systems functionalism or to accounts grounded solely in firm strategy. The EE approach provides an integrated and holistic vocabulary for describing agents, institutions, and territory-specific trajectories. At the methodological level, complex-systems thinking further reinforces the meaning of non-linearity and emergence, assigning relations and elements equal importance (Daniel et al., 2022). The EE approach can therefore support configuration-sensitive identification, helping to identify systemic change, pathways of intervention, and intra-organisational mechanisms.

The EE approach also carries stronger strategic implications when openness is treated as a constitutive feature of the ecosystem. The formation of entrepreneurial capabilities often depends on cross-regional flows of talent and other factors, as well as on extra-local value-creation structures. The external linkages of an agent organisation, namely its openness, therefore become important. Mazzoni et al. (2025) conceptualise EEs as open systems and emphasise the role of cross-regional flows of entrepreneurial talent in shaping them. Reis et al. (2022) introduce a global value-chain perspective, showing how value-adding structures and upgrading trajectories interact with ecosystem conditions and vary with the stage of industrial emergence.

Ecosystems consequently shape local performance and the spatial supply and demand of entrepreneurial agents. Reconceiving the regional context as an evolving, multi-scalar set of coordination and capability conditions has far-reaching implications for how firms organise, learn, upgrade, and expand. It also enables the EE approach to connect micro-organising and learning to meso-coordination and macro-development, with particular emphasis on the production of organisational capabilities and inter-organisational coordination within ecosystems.

Despite the rapid growth of entrepreneurship research, the EE research agenda still lacks sufficient cumulative explanation. One principal reason is the absence of consensus over ‘what EEs are’ and ‘through which mechanisms EEs evolve’. Differences in boundaries and units of enquiry make empirical studies difficult to compare, and currently reported EE effects may chiefly reflect differences in operationalisation rather than differences in underlying causal structures and mechanisms (Malecki, 2018; Miles & Morrison, 2020). Fernandes and Ferreira’s (2022) reflexive review identifies three problems: definitional proliferation, scale ambiguity, and theoretical fragmentation across relational, institutional, and organisational mechanisms.

The gap in microfoundations is especially pronounced. EE research frequently favours macro-level narratives and provides limited explanation of how entrepreneurial organising and strategising activities generate, mediate, or disrupt ecosystem evolution (Roundy & Lyons, 2023). The bidirectional influence between entrepreneurial agency and ecosystem structure remains unclear. EEs are commonly treated as contextual backgrounds and less frequently as processes constituted through situated actions. This obscures the central question of how ecosystem conditions are enacted and observed through concrete coordinating work.

Strategy as Practice (SAP) offers an opportunity to address this microfoundational gap. SAP shifts the unit of enquiry from the properties of the ecosystem to observable coordinating practices. In practice, agents mobilise resources, enact institutions, stabilise networks, and negotiate shared interpretations (Whittington, 1996; Johnson et al., 2003; Jarzabkowski et al., 2007; Vaara & Whittington, 2012). This practice turn provides a clearer answer to ‘what should be measured, and why’, connecting micro-level practices to the mechanisms and outcomes through which ecosystem capabilities are built. Connections and coordination then become performed and observable activities, extending beyond their treatment as inferred conditions in conventional EE research (Schmidt et al., 2024).
