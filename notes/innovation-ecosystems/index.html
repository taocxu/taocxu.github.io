Innovation Ecosystems

From Entrepreneurial Fanfare Back to Capabilities and Structures

28 December 2025 Tao Louie Xu

What is an innovation ecosystem in business practice? A strategist may put universities, banks, incubators, start-ups, and government agencies in a map, then counts financing rounds, patents, unicorns, and registered firms. The locality mapped suddenly appears to possess an innovation ecosystem. Certainly, I have no intention to dismiss the entrepreneurial ecosystem studies. This direction of studies well investigate the ways firms are born, grow, and mature, and it is well suited to describing interactions among entrepreneurs, investors, and market intermediaries. Yet active financing might not always surely be equal technological progress or productive-capabilities transformation. A unicorn may of course stimulate industrial agglomeration and local growth, but my enquiry is this: between the unicorn and the local innovation ecosystem, which is the chicken and which is the egg?

See it from the evolutionary economics of innovation, an innovation ecosystem is a historically grounded, institutionalised, and continuously evolving environment of selection. Heterogeneous firms, workers, users, suppliers, universities, laboratories, financial institutions, and state organisations search, learn, compete, cooperate, and are selected out within it. Knowledge is created, recombined, diffused, and industrialised, gradually becoming embedded in organisational routines, productive capabilities, and institutions.

This evolutionary economics, whether applied to institutions or innovation, cannot dispense with Thorstein Veblen. When someone calls themselves an institutional economist, further look at whom they are paying tribute to. Veblen? The German Historical School? The Austrian School? Coase, North, Williamson? Ostrom? Buchanan? Acemoglu, or someone else?

Economic evolution goes with cumulative causation. Technology may never float outside society. Institutions! Financial, organisational, managerial, technological, cultural, educational, and political ones determine which bodies of knowledge and technological trajectories obtain resources and survive. Schumpeter gave us ‘creative destruction’. New combinations enter the market, reconfigure products, processes, organisations, and industrial boundaries, and disrupt incumbent firms with established interests. An ecosystem is filled with selection, conflict, and power. This brings Marx, Parsons, Giddens, and Foucault to my mind; there’s a clear and enduring debate—does social progress emerge through conflict or through harmony? Is an ecosystem constituted by conflict or harmony? Your thought?

Nelson and Winter brought this evolutionary perspective into business organisational studies through routines, search, and selection. Firms act through established routines, whereas experimenting and searching under uncertainty. Market and institutional environments select among different technologies and organisations. Innovative capabilities accumulate over time, and today’s direction of search is constrained by what was built yesterday. This is what we call path dependence.

I particularly respect the national innovation systems studies steered by Freeman, Nelson, and Lundvall. Different countries possess different technology-policy tools and industrial organisations. Innovation is interactive learning within the diverse environments, sustained through concrete and repeated exchanges of knowledge between producers and users. Giving the universities, firms, and governments is only the starting entry. The more consequential is whether knowledge flows, feedback loops, and collective problem-solving capabilities emerge.

Many firms today seems increasingly unable to innovate through internal R&D alone. They require systemic support composed of basic research, skills formation, patient capital, infrastructure, and rules. Open innovation becomes a substantive research area, and growing attention is directed towards the environments in which it can function effectively.

Innovation ecosystems in latecomer economies must support catching-up, capability-building, and transitions in industrial leadership. Firms may first enter global value chains to learn production and operations, reduce their dependence on external lead firms, and then build design, R&D, and local supply networks. Once their capabilities mature, they may re-enter the global system from a stronger position. This ‘in-out-in’ process exhibits that internationalisation and localisation are both stage-dependent. The relevant criterion is whether firms and industries develop knowledge-creating capabilities and a locally rooted productive ecosystem.

In one important respect, infant industries and infant firms (start-ups) are closely analogous. From Friedrich List to the developmental-state literature, our community of development economists have well argued for the strategic protection and active support of infant industries when they accumulate productive capabilities. For relatively affluent and more innovation-intensive economies (although of course innovation is consequential in every economy), the contemporary institutional expression of such support consists, to a considerable extent, in constructing an innovation ecosystem.

At least for now, I regard the entrepreneurial ecosystem as one well suited within the innovation (eco)system. It delineates conditions for early experimentation and resource matching among infant firms. Yet when policy becomes absorbed in entrepreneurship competitions, the entrepreneurial ecosystem may deteriorate into ‘startup theatre’. Core technologies remain externally dependent, speculative capital remains short-termist, and relations among universities, firms, governments, and consumers remain weak.

To uncover an innovation ecosystem is to examine the means an economy produces and exchanges knowledge, organises learning, accumulates capabilities, selects technologies, distributes power, and transforms its industrial structure. It is a process, a process of co-evolution. Study the concrete micro-agents within it, as well as the macro-structures whereby their trajectories are constrained and enabled.
