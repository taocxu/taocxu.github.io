On Developmental Governance: What Digital-Asset Regulation Taught Us

2 June 2026

From legal labels to economic functions

Mohammed Shabir Korotana scrutinised digital-asset regulation across the UK, the EU, and the US. He wisely set aside the legal labels attached to a token and posed a more substantive enquiry: what activities does it actually perform?

A stablecoin facilitates payments. Custody safeguards assets on behalf of clients. A trading platform provides the market infrastructure for crypto exchange.

Activities reveal economic functions. Those functions generate, or correspond to, risks including runs, the commingling of client assets, and market manipulation. Regulatory responses therefore centre on reserves, redemption rights, asset segregation, transparency, and the market governance of disclosure, suitability, liquidity, and conduct. Legal focal points vary across jurisdictions, yet the underlying regulatory functions may converge.

When unprecedented technologies, institutions, or organisational modalities emerge, governing authorities often allocate rules according to sketchy labels, what something is called, or an imprecise category. This creates regulatory gaps, duplicated supervision, or both. A more pragmatic approach begins by examining the activities undertaken, the functions those activities reveal, the risks generated, and the design and implementation of regulatory responses.

Transferring the framework to developmental governance

Object → Activities → Productive Functions → Developmental Constraints and Political-Economic Risks Generated → Governance Responses → Compliance Burdens → Feasible Better Governance → Functional Convergence

The object is an entrance. We can identify the activities that the state undertakes through a particular policy instrument, the productive functions embodied in those activities, the constraints they are intended to mitigate, and the political-economic risks simultaneously generated.

Filippo Marchetti, discussing the risk-based approach, indicates that governance intensity should adjust according to the probability, severity, and coverage of relevant risks. Ilias Kapsis, addressing legal simplification, reminds us that risk governance can itself generate fixed compliance costs, repetitive procedures, and interpretive conflicts. These burdens can restrict entry, experimentation, and expansion. The burdens created by rules per se therefore also become an object of scrutiny.

The governance paradox of industrial policy

In industrial-policy studies, I see a governance paradox. When policy rents are misused, projects fail, or corruption occurs, governments typically add written approvals and audit procedures so that responsibility can be attached to a particular signatory. A failure produces another rule, apparently deliberate yet fundamentally clichéd. Over time, the rules constitute an increasingly elaborate compliance system that erodes developmental agency.

An alternative response often falls back on the neoliberal ‘good governance’ agenda that dominated the previous three decades. China’s industrial-policy practice suggests a different line of enquiry. I use industrial investment, public procurement, and special economic zones, including contemporary development zones, as three illustrative cases.

Three illustrative policy instruments

Industrial investment

Industrial investment through industrial funds provides patient capital through angel investment, venture capital, public-private partnerships, which are often discussed in infrastructure studies, and staged financing. Patient capital is certainly not a cutting-edge concept. It appears extensively in the work of Justin Yifu Lin, one of my long-standing academic heroes.

‘Hey, company, you’re not alone.’

Such investment can share responsibility for managing technological uncertainty and coordinate industrial chains. It may also socialise costs and create severe exit difficulties. On coordination failure and uncertainty, Mariana Mazzucato is a must-read. So are Paul Rosenstein-Rodan, Ragnar Nurkse, Albert Hirschman, Russell Cooper, Andrew John, Kiminori Matsuyama, and Dani Rodrik.

Feasible governance must attach follow-on investment to productive-capabilities transformation.

‘Let’s go! From the lab to the market.’

The market is the gym in which the ‘productive capabilities’ muscle is built. Competition, private capital, and risk allocation must all remain on the table. We should closely scrutinise investments, transactions, and projects that remain indefinitely unexited. From Amsden, Wade, and Evans to Khan, the underlying lesson is clear: embedded autonomy entails discipline. Conditions should be attached to learning, exports, and today’s diverse modalities of innovation. Their precise form depends on the mission the state is pursuing.

Mission-oriented public procurement

The same logic applies to mission-oriented procurement within the entrepreneurial state, with due acknowledgement to Mariana Mazzucato. Through contract design, public procurement organises demand and technology, creates initial markets, and helps firms overcome demand uncertainty and minimum-scale barriers.

The risks are equally serious. In practice, I have encountered numerous stories of collusion between officials and businesses, bid rigging, local protectionism, and the exclusion of smaller firms by dominant corporations.

Special economic zones and development zones

Special economic zones and development zones concentrate scarce state capabilities through infrastructure policy, land-use policy, tax policy, trade policy, and related interventions. They resemble cities such as Hong Kong and London, which serve as financial hubs and gateways connecting East Asia and Western Europe to the global capital system.

Drawing on the concepts of global production networks and global value chains, SEZs and development zones connect a region to the global economy through a particular locality.

Alongside Hirschman and Krugman, I also attend to Li-An Zhou, Wei Xiong, and Yuen Yuen Ang. Behind agglomeration and productive linkages lies the possibility of slippage into land-property finance, as I discuss in ‘Why Cities Backfire? High-Speed Railway New Town Planning and Urban Structural Change’, and subsidy tournaments. Policy interventions should be tied to the realisation of investment commitments, technological capabilities, employment, and other missions. They should also carry time limits and credible exit rules.

Feasible better governance

According to Khan’s political settlements framework, the conception of feasible better governance can be made more explicit. Under a given power structure, set of organisational capabilities, and configuration of policy rents, the mission is to select an institutional arrangement that mitigates critical constraints and manages the most consequential rents pragmatically.

Three propositions

  1. Where states employ formally different policy instruments that perform similar productive functions and generate comparable risks, similarity in governance functions may exceed similarity in formal institutional design.

  2. Among policy activities performing comparable functions, greater procedural duplication and higher compliance costs tend to concentrate policy benefits in larger, more mature organisations possessing stronger political resources. Corruption in the Global South receives extensive attention. Lobbying deserves equal scrutiny. We should identify the larger, more mature, and politically advantaged organisations to determine whether the underlying processes are functionally convergent.

  3. What do I mean by discipline? Performance conditions and exit rules become substantive only when the state possesses the capability to suspend, and where justified withdraw, policy rents while withstanding discontent from beneficiary organisations with established interests. Embedded autonomy matters. Clientelism studies likewise remind us that governments in some Global South economies struggle to withstand such discontent across both more and less democratic systems.

Examine what a policy package, or one measure within it, actually does. This framework makes the enquiry more explicit.
