Developmentalism: Capitalist Transformation, State Power, and Ideology

Reading Notes | 8 November 2026

Graham Harrison (2020), Developmentalism: The Normative and Transformative within Capitalism. Oxford.

1. Development as Capitalist Transformation

Development must be understood within the history of capitalist development. Read the foundations in the transformation of productive forces and relations of production—property, labour, class relations, technological capabilities, and sustained capital accumulation.

Economic growth and poverty reduction are important outcomes, but sustained development requires deeper structural transformation. Yes, Critical Realism—the causal significance of historically constituted social relations, moving beyond observable outcomes to examine the underlying structures that generate them.

Capitalist transformation creates the material conditions for widespread improvements in living standards, simultaneously, generating exploitation, dispossession, and social conflict. Whether the productive possibilities translate into collective welfare remains a political question.

2. Developmentalism as Political Project

Developmentalism is conceptualised as a long-term political project through which states organise sustained capital accumulation and transformative socioeconomic change.

  1. State-Capital Relations: Organising investment, accumulation, and productive transformation.
  2. Ruling Elites: Maintaining political coordination and governing coalitions.
  3. Ideologies of Statehood: Constructing political purpose, national identity, and legitimacy.
  4. Resilience of Rule: Sustaining political authority through crises.
  5. Popular Mobilisation and Contestation: Negotiating the distribution of development benefits and pursuing Commonwealth.

Development can become an existential necessity of the state, particularly under pressures concerning sovereignty, security and political survival. Crucially, political legitimacy has material foundations. The state authority depends partly on its capacity to organise production, mobilise resources, and tackle poverty/material deprivation.

3. Historical Development and Contradictions

Across the cases, capitalist transformation emerges through changing relations among state institutions, capital, labour, ideology, and collective agency. National development projects are under specific histories of industrialisation, state formation, geopolitical competition and social struggle.

The language of economic ‘miracles’ can obscure the material processes and their associated coercion, dispossession, and inequalities. Developmental achievements must be assessed alongside their political practices, social costs and distributional consequences.

Harrison consequently advocates a historically grounded understanding of development that recognises economic improvement, political stability, individual rights, national sovereignty and collective welfare as potentially competing values.

4. My Reflections: Ideology and the Political Economy of Development

Harrison offers a compelling framework for understanding the structural and political foundations of development. My particular interest concerns the causal efficacy of developmental ideology.

How developmental(ist) ideas emerge, acquire legitimacy, and become embedded in institutions? Under what conditions can they direct collective agency, coordinate economic actors/agents, and contribute to productive transformation? Which may reinforce one another, but their causal relationships require empirical investigation.

Developmentalism connects capitalist structural transformation with state power, political legitimacy, and collective agency. Understanding how developmental ideologies acquire practical and productive effects remains a particularly important question for our political economy of development.
