How Industrial Policy Builds State Capacity? Precondition or Endogenous Formation?

Research Note · 4 October 2026

1. Reversing a Familiar Question

A familiar question in industrial policy research is whether a state possesses sufficient administrative capacity to identify industries, allocate resources, coordinate investment, monitor firms, and implement policy measures. This has long been central to debates about industrial policy efficacy. Yet understanding state capacity as a pre-existing condition leaves an important dynamic process unexplored, i.e. the practice of industrial policy may itself transform the state’s capacity to undertake subsequent interventions.

This is where I start Learning by Doing Industrial Policy. My argument, this, situates state capacity within the process of economic development, tracing the ways and means public organisations acquire knowledge, establish administrative routines, and accumulate capabilities through the practical challenges of industrial transformation.

The central proposition is straightforward. Industrial policy can build the state capacity it requires. Policy implementation creates opportunities for organisational learning; whether the opportunities produce lasting capabilities may rely on incentives, institutions, the ways public organisations learn from experience—and of course the ways all of the agents understand the incentives, institutions, and themselves.

2. From Productive Powers to the Capabilities of the State

Alexander Hamilton (1791) and Friedrich List (1841) highlighted the cultivation of national productive powers. Industrialisation requires the historical accumulation of technologies, skills, infrastructure, and productive organisations. Gerschenkron (1962) demonstrated the ways late-industrialising economies could develop different institutions to overcome shortages of capital, knowledge, and coordination.

Their contributions illuminate that late-developing economies can develop productive capabilities. A closely related question concerns the public organisations responsible for facilitating that development. How do the institutions that organise industrialisation acquire their own capabilities to do this?

Firms learn to manufacture products; and public agencies may learn to identify production bottlenecks, coordinate institutions, and assess policy outcomes through repeated engagement with industrial projects. The processes are interconnected, although the learning undertaken by firms, financial institutions, and government agencies should remain analytically distinct.

The literature on state formation is a further point of departure. Tilly (1985) examined why war-making, extraction, and related state activities contributed to the historical formation of state organisation and coercive capacities. Besley and Persson (2009) analysed the political and economic incentives underlying investments in legal and fiscal state capacity. Their perspectives suggest three related pathways through which state capacities develop: state activities generate demands for organisational expansion; governments deliberately invest in administrative capabilities; and public organisations accumulate knowledge through performing concrete tasks.

The present argument centres particularly on the third pathway: capability formation through policy practice. Tilly’s research on European state formation concerned coercion, extraction, and war-making; industrial policy raised a distinct issue about the ways developmental tasks generate organisational learning.

This requires a further conceptual distinction. State capacity refers to the state’s ability to pursue and implement public objectives. Organisational capabilities concern what particular public bodies can reliably accomplish through their personnel, routines, knowledge, and institutional relationships. Industrial policy capabilities are the subset relevant to designing, coordinating, monitoring, and altering interventions in productive development.

3. Developmental State as Evolving Organisational Relationship

The developmental-state literature, particularly Amsden (1989), Wade (1990), and Evans (1995), situated industrial upgrading within the organisational relationships connecting states and firms.

Evans’s concept of embedded autonomy is especially relevant. Effective public intervention depends on close engagement with productive actors, through which officials acquire information about industrial constraints, alongside sufficient organisational autonomy to pursue public objectives and enforce policy discipline. Information, coordination, incentives, and accountability work together.

From a dynamic perspective, embedded autonomy is itself something that public organisations may have to develop. Officials acquire knowledge through repeated interaction with firms. Government departments establish coordination procedures as they resolve disputes and implementation problems. Public agencies revise their assessments through policy feedback and accumulated experience.

Such experience becomes a durable organisational capability. How? Research on organisational learning, particularly Levitt and March (1988), provides a useful mechanism. Organisations can encode lessons from experience into routines that subsequently guide behaviour. Applied to industrial policy, this suggests a sequence:

Industrial Policy Practice → Organisational Learning → Capability Accumulation or Erosion → Subsequent Policy Capacity

The distinction between individual learning and organisational learning is essential. Experience accumulated by individual policymakers does not automatically survive personnel turnover. Durable capability formation requires mechanisms through which organisations retain, communicate, and reuse knowledge.

The same process can also generate adverse outcomes. Repeated interactions may entrench dependence on particular firms, reinforce ineffective routines, or expose public agencies to political capture. Organisational learning may produce greater adaptability, institutional rigidity, or even the erosion of existing capabilities.

State capacity, in this sense, should be examined through the capabilities that particular public organisations can actually mobilise for particular tasks. These capabilities reside in people, procedures, information systems, organisational interfaces, and relations of authority.

The success of an industrial project demonstrates an economic outcome. Whether the responsible public organisations have acquired enduring capabilities requires a further examination of what they can accomplish afterwards.

4. An Open Research Agenda: When Industrial Policy Builds State Capacity?

Industrial policy and state capacity are connected through a potentially recursive relationship. Existing capabilities determine the selection, design, and implementation of industrial interventions. Policy implementation generates organisational experiences that may alter those capabilities. The resulting organisational changes, in turn, influence the state’s capacity to undertake future interventions. This process may expand a state’s scope for developmental action, but it may also reproduce ineffective practices and institutional dependencies.

An important research task is to identify the conditions under which policy experience becomes organisational knowledge, and organisational knowledge becomes a capability that can be sustained and redeployed.

Do public agencies become better at identifying industrial constraints after successive interventions? Can coordination procedures developed for one project be applied to subsequent projects or other sectors? Do improvements survive personnel changes? Does accumulated experience strengthen the state’s ability to monitor firms, revise policy instruments, and manage competing interests?

The research agenda can be expressed through a more precise question:

Which industrial policy practices enable which public organisations to acquire which capabilities that can be sustained, transferred, and reused?

The broader implication is that state capacity can be understood as both a condition and a potential outcome of industrial development. Understanding this reciprocal process may explain how developmental institutions emerge, evolve, and sometimes fail through the very activities they undertake.
