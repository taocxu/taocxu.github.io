Rwanda’s Rise and the Limits of Structural Transformation

9 October 2026 Tao Louie Xu

At a SOAS book launch chaired by Phil Clark, Pritish Behuria discussed The Political Economy of Rwanda’s Rise (Cambridge University Press, 2026). His central puzzle is compelling: how can a country achieve economic growth and export diversification without sustained structural transformation?

Since 1994, Rwanda has made important gains in public health, exports, and international services. Yet broad-based technological capabilities among domestic firms and productive employment have proved harder to cultivate. Behuria connects these uneven outcomes to political power, the allocation of capital, and the difficulties of sustained economic learning.

1. Growth, Diversification, and Productive Capabilities

Behuria distinguishes three outcomes. Economic growth captures expanding output and income; export diversification broadens goods, services, and foreign-exchange earnings; structural transformation entails lasting advances in firms’ productive capabilities, skills, labour productivity, and employment.

The third requires firms to invest, experiment, learn, and sometimes fail. Governments must be able to provide patient finance, support learning, and discipline investment. Rwanda has diversified, but its domestic productive base has developed less extensively. Services may contribute to transformation, provided they generate skills, productive jobs, and durable domestic linkages.

2. From Landlocked Economy to Services Hub

Landlocked geography raises transport costs and accentuates Rwanda’s historical dependence on volatile commodity exports, particularly coffee. After 1994, the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) embraced self-reliance and sought to turn a geographical constraint into an ambition to become a land-linked regional hub.

Tourism, conferences, aviation, hospitality, and nation branding became central to this strategy. RwandAir and international events brought visibility and new sources of foreign exchange. Behuria takes this services-first approach seriously, while asking whether flagship projects develop local suppliers, transferable skills, and employment beyond the projects themselves.

3. Political Settlements Across Borders

Drawing on political settlements analysis, particularly Mushtaq Khan’s work, Behuria emphasises elite vulnerability: an independent domestic capitalist class may acquire resources capable of financing political opposition.

He traces the RPF’s changing settlement from the redistribution of assets and offices after 1994, through the centralisation of political and economic control, to renewed elite reconfiguration around the withdrawal of donor support in 2012. Some former insiders became opponents abroad, giving political contestation an increasingly transnational character.

Rwanda’s international reputation, regional relations, and development finance are closely connected. A services hub depends on foreign investors, visitors, and partners, while political controversy and conflict involving the Democratic Republic of the Congo may jeopardise its external standing. The pursuit of political stability can also weaken sustained collaboration with independent domestic firms.

4. Financing Investment and Learning

Rwanda’s investment system extends beyond party- and military-linked enterprises such as Crystal Ventures and Horizon. The Rwanda Social Security Board (RSSB), the Development Bank of Rwanda, and other vehicles have mobilised domestic funds for strategic projects.

These institutions can act as functional substitutes for scarce long-term development finance. Yet capital mobilisation alone cannot secure industrial upgrading. Effective rents for learning require consistent support, performance discipline, room for failure, and cumulative firm-level learning. Political reshuffles and external financiers’ limited tolerance for risk can interrupt that process.

5. Evidence Across Sectors

Agriculture. Coffee and tea upgrading, agro-processing, and new exports have expanded production. MINIMEX and Inyange Industries illustrate the role of domestic demand, procurement, and supply-chain co-ordination, alongside unequal access to land and resources.

Mining. Tin, tantalum, and tungsten bring foreign exchange. Cross-border trade linked to the DRC raises questions about mineral sourcing, conflict, political networks, and the prospects for domestic beneficiation.

Services. Tourism and aviation generate international visibility, but their developmental contribution depends on domestic sourcing, freight costs, skills, and employment.

Manufacturing. Projects involving Volkswagen and BioNTech can introduce investment and technology; wider transformation requires enduring supplier relationships, technological learning, and productive jobs.

Across these sectors, the central issue remains which domestic firms receive support, what capabilities they acquire, and whether learning translates into sustained employment.

After the Seminar

Two propositions stayed with me. First, state intervention and productive capability-building can diverge: ambitious investment projects may coexist with weak domestic learning. Second, industrial policy is inseparable from the politics of capital allocation. The stability of the governing coalition shapes which firms receive resources, the conditions attached to support, and whether learning can continue.

Rwanda thus offers a revealing account of how domestic political settlements and transnational economic pressures jointly shape the possibilities of late development.
