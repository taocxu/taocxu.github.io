Learning by Doing Industrial Policy: How Governments Learn from Policy Practice?

Research Note · 6 October 2026

1. Practice Generates Experience, Learning Requires Transformation

Arrow’s (1962) concept of learning by doing demonstrates that productive activity itself can generate knowledge and improvements in efficiency. Extend this insight to industrial policy. Can governments also learn through the practical implementation of their policy practice? The answer requires a distinction between three interconnected concepts: experience, organisational learning, and state organisational capability.

A public agency may repeatedly assess applications for industrial subsidies and encounter hundreds of projects, yet its judgement may remain unchanged. We have reason to speak of learning when these experiences alter the ways officials understand problems, make collective decisions, or carry out administrative procedures. Learning develops into a durable organisational capability when such changes are retained within the organisation and can be mobilised in subsequent tasks.

2. Why Industrial Policy Particularly Conducive to Learning?

Hirschman’s (1958) theory of development highlighted unbalanced growth, linkages, and the decisions induced by emerging development problems. Industrial transformation reveals bottlenecks that cannot always be fully anticipated. A firm may secure financing but lack reliable intermediate inputs. Different public agencies may pursue conflicting objectives through their approval procedures. These difficulties induce actors to seek information, revise strategies, and organise collective responses.

Industrial policy performs an important epistemic function. It makes problems embedded in productive structures and organisational relationships visible. Sustained engagement with firms provides access to dispersed knowledge; project implementation generates feedback; and failures and disagreements compel public organisations to reconsider their assumptions. Hirschman’s work on induced problem-solving offers an important foundation in development economics for understanding that governments may learn by doing.

3. How Knowledge Becomes Organisational Capability?

Penrose (1959) spotlighted the relationship between firm growth, managerial experience, and collective knowledge. Nelson and Winter (1982) introduced routines into evolutionary economics to explain how knowledge becomes embedded in organisational behaviour. Zollo and Winter (2002) further distinguished experience accumulation, knowledge articulation, and knowledge codification. The concepts are useful for understanding learning within public organisations.

Policy Implementation Experience → Information Testing and Collective Interpretation → Adjustment of Procedures and Organisational Relationships → Knowledge Retention → Reuse in Future Tasks

Levitt and March (1988) remind us that historical experience frequently conditions present organisational behaviour through established routines. Yet organisations may also preserve judgements and practices that have become obsolete.

The value of organisational memory consequently depends on whether accumulated knowledge can be retrieved, revised, and recombined.

4. Government Learning Is Political

Learning within public organisations is also conditioned by power, interests, and relations of authority. Who has the authority to define policy failure? Whose evidence is recognised as legitimate? Who decides which lessons should become institutionalised?

Ang’s (2016) concept of directed improvisation and Andrews, Pritchett, and Woolcock’s (2013) Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA) encourage us to understand learning through organisational incentives, experimentation, adaptation, and feedback. Government learning by doing involves the accumulation of technical knowledge alongside the organisational and political processes through which knowledge is interpreted, selected, and used.

The central question is policy practice gradually develops public organisational capabilities that can be mobilised, revised, and sustained across successive rounds of industrialisation.
