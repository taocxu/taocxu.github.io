International Development: Growth, Structural Change, and State Capacity

Notes on the VoxDev seminar ‘The State of International Development’

4 November 2026 | Rohini Pande (Yale University), on the Handbook of Development Economics, Volume 6, co-edited with Pascaline Dupas and Pinelopi Koujianou Goldberg

Rohini Pande reviewed the achievements of development since the late 1980s and the changing conditions for future progress. Extreme poverty fell from approximately 2.3 billion people in 1989 to 850 million in 2023 (using the US$3-a-day poverty line at 2021 purchasing power parity), largely through gains in East Asia and, later, South Asia. Life expectancy and child survival improved across income levels, while schooling, political rights and social protection expanded. Yet poverty reduction has slowed, and progress remains uneven, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa.

The discussion centred on what obstructs further development, where will future growth come from, and how can that growth be broadly shared?

Changing Constraints on Development

Conflict, climate risks, democratic backsliding, weak state capacity, and a less predictable international economic order are making development more difficult. East Asia’s export-driven industrialisation benefited from access to large markets, participation in global value chains, learning by doing, and technological upgrading. For many African economies, high trade costs, limited integration into manufacturing value chains and renewed protectionism constrain the scope for following that trajectory.

Pande highlighted the role of the state. Markets operate within political institutions; governments are needed to provide security, public goods and credible enforcement. This set of functions is vital especially when conflict and external shocks threaten investment and production.

Where Might Future Growth Come From?

Two possibilities featured prominently.

  • Technology adoption: Existing technologies can raise productivity without frontier innovation. Their diffusion nevertheless depends on finance, skills, suitable technologies and effective institutions.
  • Service-based growth: Productivity improvements within services can drive aggregate growth. This differs from service-biased growth, in which productivity gains elsewhere increase incomes and consequently demand for services. Evidence from parts of Africa and India supports service-led growth, while its capacity to generate the wider spillovers historically associated with manufacturing remains an open question.

In a slower-growth environment, resource allocation becomes more consequential. The literature describes wedges as distortions separating actual allocations of capital, labour and other resources from a frictionless benchmark. Reducing misallocation, improving service quality and addressing barriers such as gender discrimination can raise the productivity of existing resources.

Growth for Whom?

Aggregate growth has not always produced secure employment or convergence in living standards. Service gains may also accrue disproportionately to urban and wealthier households. A substantial share of the world’s extreme poor now live in middle-income countries, and informal employment has remained persistent despite decades of growth. Expanding schooling has likewise yielded much clearer gains in access than in learning quality. Improvements in women’s legal rights and educational attainment have not fully translated into labour-market equality.

The patterns underscore the importance of evaluating development through productive employment, learning and distributional outcomes, alongside aggregate income.
