Golgeci et al. (2025) see *power asymmetry* as an intrinsic feature of *global supply chains*. Analysing highly politicised and organisationally complex chains, powerful MNEs, states, and international organisations often dominate the rules of participation. They influence the way value is distributed, whose voices are heard, and where risks are absorbed or displaced. This sociopolitical perspective identifies the principal expressions of unequal power, but it may also raise a prior question: through what material and institutional mechanisms are the asymmetries (re)produced?

**Infrastructure.** It establishes the conditions under which weaker participants can enter a chain, remain connected, and bargain over value.

## **I. Understanding Infrastructure: The Conditions of Production and Exchange**

Infrastructure comprises the conditions connecting production and exchange. Its visible components include roads, railways, air routes, shipping lanes, ports, warehouses, and cold-chain facilities. Its less visible components include technical standards, operating protocols, data systems, and governance rules.

Conditions of connection. Audretsch et al. (2015) argue that infrastructure connects people to people, firms to markets, and knowledge to opportunities, helping economic actors identify opportunities and enter markets. Applied to supply chains, these connections determine whether producers can reach buyers, preserve product quality, and participate in exchange.

Visible and invisible infrastructure. Tandrayen-Ragoobur et al. (2023) show that regional trade in Africa depends on procedures and rules as well as roads and ports: procedures make physical connection usable for trade. Both material commodity exchange and digital exchange therefore rely on visible and invisible infrastructures (Adams et al., 2024).

Publicness and *common production*. Park et al. (2024) conceptualise enabling infrastructure as a shared platform that is collectively used and governed. Connectivity acquires a public and enabling character when participants can enter the procedures through which access, standards, and operating rules are determined.

Infrastructure is therefore a set of material and institutional conditions for production and exchange. It can enable common economic life, but only when access and governance extend beyond physical construction to the rules that organise how connection works.

## **II. Conditional Connection: How Infrastructure Produces Space and Dependency**

Infrastructure does more than connect pre-existing places. Through investment, construction, and governance, it actively produces regional space, selects some actors and locations for connection, and creates relations of dependence.

Regional formation and exclusion. Glass et al. (2019) describe ‘*infrastructural regionalism*’: regions are made through the planning, financing, construction, and governance of infrastructure. Selected cities and industries become intensively connected and may form development coalitions (Wachsmuth, 2017), while other places are marginalised or bypassed by the same networks.

Financialisation and debt dependence. Furlong (2020) shows that infrastructure is commonly embedded in debt and *financialisation*, with present construction financed against future revenues. These arrangements can concentrate power and generate environmental and financial risks. Investors may design projects around their own return requirements, while host-country elites also derive economic and political benefits. Tawakkol (2026) consequently links infrastructure financialisation to intensified relations of subordination.

Geopolitics. Furlong (2022) observes that China’s Belt and Road infrastructure can create substantive opportunities across the global South while also embedding debt relations and dependence on technical standards. Zhao (2023) places both physical and digital infrastructure within Sino-US geopolitical and technological competition.

Actors that control debt, ownership, and technical standards influence which places are connected, which are bypassed, and whose strategic purposes infrastructure serves.

### **1. Infrastructure Determines the Conditions of Supply-Chain Participation**

### **2. Participation Is Conditional: Its Conditions Produce Space and Dependency**

Smallholders and smaller firms require infrastructure to enter global markets, maintain quality, move goods, receive finance, and comply with technical requirements.

Yet these actors cannot independently build roads, ports, cold chains, payment systems, or cross-border standards. They must rely on actors that finance, own, construct, or govern them. Furlong (2020) describes the resulting relationship as a form of ‘*power at a distance*’: decisions taken by investors and infrastructure authorities structure the choices available to local producers. Financialisation may deepen this dependence by aligning infrastructure operation with creditor and investor interests (Tawakkol, 2026).

### **3. Dependency Concentrates Power and Produces Asymmetry**

A small number of multinational enterprises, government agencies, financiers, and international organisations may control infrastructure debt, ownership, technical standards, and access rules. Control over infrastructure thereby becomes control over supply-chain participation.

To control infrastructure is to influence rules, value, voice, and risk. Following Golgeci et al. (2025), supply-chain power asymmetry can be traced through four connected dimensions:

**Rule-setting power:** powerful actors define access and operating requirements, while weaker participants bear the costs of compliance.

**Value-capture and distributional power:** ownership, debt claims, fees, and bargaining advantages allow stronger actors to capture a disproportionate share of the returns generated through the chain.

**Voice and representational power:** weaker participants are frequently absent from the forums in which infrastructure priorities, standards, accountability, and remedies are determined.

**Risk-allocation power:** stronger actors use contracts, information advantages, and infrastructural control to transfer transport, price, delay, quality, environmental, and geopolitical risks towards weaker participants.
