## Infrastructure as Public Good with Public Value

Research in public economics on *public goods* and public investment provides the classical theoretical point of departure for discussions of infrastructure. As a public good, infrastructure is characterised by high fixed costs, durability, and strong network externalities. Transport, energy, communications, education, and healthcare generate social returns from investment that exceed private costs (Samuelson, 1954; Buchanan, 1965; Ostrom, 1990/2015; Conrad & Seitz, 1994; Kaul et al., 1999).

In regions of the Global South where capital is relatively dispersed, complete reliance on private investment and development for these public goods often produces under-provision and quality gaps, leading to traffic congestion, energy waste, poor communications, and related problems. Private speculation tends to pursue short-term windfall profits, a tendency that departs from the public-good properties of infrastructure.

Infrastructure functions by supplying continuous ‘*service flows*’ that facilitate the movement of other agents, resources, goods, and services (Aschauer, 1989; Gramlich, 1994; Graham & Marvin, 2001; Edwards, 2002; Haughwout, 2002; Larkin, 2013). High investment yields long-term returns across time and space, yet the prolonged process of value realisation also gives rise to governance difficulties involving ‘(quasi-)public goods’, ‘common-pool resources’, and the ‘tragedy of the commons’ (Samuelson, 1954; Buchanan, 1965; Ostrom, 1990; Star, 1999; Flyvbjerg et al., 2002; Meynhardt, 2009; Frischmann, 2012).

From this perspective, institutional and practical debates over the governance of infrastructure’s ‘*public value*’ have shifted from ‘whether government should provide it’ towards questions of how infrastructure should be developed, maintained, opened, excluded, or obstructed (Bakonyi & Darwich, 2025). Public value theory indicates that the study of infrastructure must extend beyond efficiency and cost to balance norms including accessibility, inclusiveness, accountability, and procedural justice (Bryson et al., 2014; Addie et al., 2019; Vuorinen & Martinsuo, 2019; Sims, 2021; Mazzucato et al., 2024).

Public value, meaning ‘what the public regards as valuable’ (Vuorinen & Martinsuo, 2019), is commonly realised through concrete forms of infrastructure of different kinds.

**1. Transport infrastructure is a typical ‘hard asset’.** It includes roads, railways, ports, air routes, and logistics facilities such as cold-chain warehousing. Its public value lies chiefly in reducing transaction costs, improving transport efficiency and market accessibility, and promoting regional specialisation (Eberts & McMillen, 1999; Limão & Venables, 2001; Clark et al., 2004; Leduc & Wilson, 2012; Donaldson, & Hornbeck, 2016; Coşar & Demir, 2016; Diao et al., 2017; Aljuneidi et al., 2024).

**2. Energy and utility circuits,** ranging from the supply of electricity, gas, and clean water to utilities such as sewage-treatment systems, sustain everyday production and life and shape social cost structures, economic equity, and sustainable development (Kaul et al., 1999; Ndulu, 2006; Frischmann, 2012; Mains, 2012; Meehan, 2014; Jambadu et al., 2024).

**3. Communications and digital infrastructure** includes 5G, fibre broadband, satellite internet, payment systems, and public information platforms. Through standards, interfaces, and data governance, it secures information flows, strengthens organisational coordination, and shapes the boundaries of public services and market transactions (Graham & Marvin, 2001; Klievink et al., 2015; Lin, 2022; Rukanova et al., 2023; Acosta & Baldomero-Quintana, 2024; Mazzucato et al., 2024).

**4. Education, training, and research and development carry greater strategic significance.** Their value lies in strengthening human capital and knowledge-production capabilities, enabling peripheral regions to acquire stronger bargaining power within regional specialisation and helping lagging regions alter their growth trajectories through knowledge and technology (Pietrobelli & Rabellotti, 2011; Perez-Sebastian & Steinbuks, 2017; Atolia et al., 2021).

**5. Healthcare and public-health infrastructure** is central to safeguarding community resilience and resisting risk. It directly affects labour reproduction, crisis management, and the stability of social order (Kaul et al., 1999; Small & Rodgers, 2023).

From a governance perspective, the realisation of infrastructure’s public value is commonly embedded in multi-actor coordination structures. Governments, firms, and social organisations continuously negotiate accessibility, inclusiveness, efficiency, effectiveness, and distributive justice, while public value constitutes the ultimate performance objective of infrastructure governance (Meynhardt, 2009; Bryson et al., 2014; Klievink et al., 2015; Rukanova et al., 2023; Mazzucato et al., 2024).

Vuorinen and Martinsuo (2019) emphasise that infrastructure projects are sites where public value intersects with the influence of commercial stakeholders. Embedding goal setting, option selection, and rule trade-offs within project management helps realise public value (see Engel et al., 2013; Klievink et al., 2015). Discussions of digital public infrastructure take the public-good attribute further into the question of ‘what is public’, including governance structures organised around inclusiveness, accessibility and re-accessibility, and accountability (Frischmann, 2012; Rukanova et al., 2023; Mazzucato et al., 2024).

Public value can coexist with commercial logic. Tensions between public-value objectives and financial constraints mean that infrastructure is often operated through user charges, concessions, PPPs, and related arrangements (Engel, et al., 2013; O’Neill, 2013; Bryson et al., 2014; Klievink et al., 2015; Vuorinen & Martinsuo, 2019; Fay et al., 2021).

Viewed through its effects, public value also entails a series of production-enhancing mechanisms. Macroeconomic and regional-economic research treats infrastructure as public capital that improves productivity and welfare. Such public goods accelerate factor mobility by lowering transaction costs, while agglomeration and network externalities advance market expansion and structural transformation (Eberts & McMillen, 1999; Haughwout, 2002; Bougheas et al., 2003; Francois et al., 2009; Donaldson & Hornbeck, 2016; Perez-Sebastian & Steinbuks, 2017; Khan et al., 2025). Put simply, infrastructure coordinates production, reduces production costs, and raises and diffuses productivity.

In the context of the Global South, these mechanisms take the form of developing ‘channels’ and ‘capabilities’. Transport systems and energy networks directly shape logistics efficiency and market access, helping small firms and small communities in small economies enter larger markets. Education, training, and research and development determine the bargaining space available to the South in pursuing value-chain upgrading (Ndulu, 2006; Mbekeani, 2010; Perez-Sebastian & Steinbuks, 2017; Donaldson, 2018; Atolia et al., 2021; Lawhon et al., 2023; Chatterjee et al., 2025).

Public value derives precisely from infrastructure’s durability and network externalities, which make possible production enhancement, structural transformation, and the realisation of higher value on the basis of long-term service flows. Infrastructure therefore functions as a public good and as a carrier of public value, shaping the position and development trajectory of the Global South within value chains through different material forms and ‘soft’ institutions.

For Southern economies, however, a sharper question follows: **who represents the public?** Infrastructure mobilises public value by facilitating the movement of other actors and resources. Does it simultaneously stimulate the valorisation of private capital and generate pronounced spatial differentiation or inequality?

## Infrastructure as Capitalist Power in Profit Extraction

The crux of the governance difficulty lies in infrastructure’s role beyond a material asset. As a public good, it mobilises processes of value creation, distribution, and extraction; it constitutes a complex ‘*sociotechnical system*’ and a material expression of capitalist power (Edwards, 2002; Larkin, 2013; Lawhon et al. 2023; Bakonyi et al., 2025). This represents a major turn in the study of infrastructure across political economy, sociology, and anthropology since the beginning of the new century (compare Samuelson, Buchanan, and Aschauer).

Ethnographic research on sociotechnical systems understands infrastructure as a composite of technology, institutions, and organisational practices that shapes sociotechnical order. Its operation conceals maintenance labour and hegemonic exclusion, which remain invisible in everyday routines and become visible through breakdowns and disputes (Star, 1999; Mains, 2012; Larkin, 2013; Berlant, 2016; Mavelli, 2024).

Critical reflection in political economy argues that infrastructure solidifies ‘modernity’ and embeds temporal, spatial, and natural orders within capitalism. Infrastructure assets consequently appear neutral while silently determining resource allocation and eligibility to participate in ways that remain unseen by the public (O’Neill, 2013; Meehan, 2014; Ranganathan, 2019; Lin, 2022; Bakonyi & Darwich, 2025). Research on sociotechnical complexes reveals the capitalist power behind seemingly neutral rules of technical governance by exposing questions of ‘exclusion’, ‘distribution’, and ‘participation’, namely, who bears the costs and who benefits.

Infrastructure provides the underlying conditions for profit extraction and capitalist reproduction. As a public good, it also accelerates the speed and scale of private capital circulation, binding the organisation of corporate production and the state’s governance of territorial space to the same set of material assets (Ranganathan, 2019; Szabó & Jelinek, 2023; Bakonyi & Darwich, 2025). Bakonyi et al. (2025) argue that, through standardisation and modularisation, infrastructure translates time, space, and nature into units that can be valued and invested in, inscribing promises of profitability onto spatial infrastructural forms (Larkin, 2013; Darwich & Bakonyi, 2025).

In Bakonyi and Darwich’s reflection, the ‘*power of capital*’ identified by Marx is a form of social coercion directed towards capital accumulation. It first holds workers’ wages below the value of their labour in production, thereby extracting surplus value; it then realises value as money in circulation and returns profit to the next round of investment and accumulation. In the sphere of circulation, money and commodities move across space through numerous facilities, assets, and regions. The power of capital therefore places particular emphasis on the turnover efficiency of corridors.

By connecting, directing, and blocking flows, infrastructure turns profit realisation and capital accumulation into an ongoing process of expansion. Capitalism and infrastructure development are inseparable because capitalist reproduction proceeds through circulation and cyclical turnover, a process that depends on the spatial material assets of infrastructure. Infrastructure facilitates circulation and, in turn, transforms the mode of capitalist reproduction, altering our understanding of state territorial power. Capital circulation thereby introduces new means of transregional coordination and risk transfer (O’Neill, 2013; Ranganathan, 2019).

Furthermore, infrastructure supplies service flows to markets and relies on the remaking of financial channels, hubs, and transactional boundaries to create differentiated accessibility and profit opportunities across regions and groups (Behrens, 2011; Addie et al., 2019). Such differentiation is often packaged within development narratives and imaginaries of modernity. Vested interests are more likely to monopolise the advantages of access to capital, efficiently extracting wealth and entrenching inequality through capitalist reproduction across investment, production, and circulation.

The *financialisation* of infrastructure is central to capitalist power’s extraction of profit. O’Neill (2013) argues that financialisation transforms infrastructure into a tradable asset through classification, valuation, and contracts. It locks stable returns in for capital markets while transferring risks to public finance and the public. When infrastructure is redefined as an investible asset, capital can obtain relatively stable long-term cash-flow returns by manipulating property relations and structures of revenue distribution, while changing the project’s pricing logic and allocation of risk (Flyvbjerg et al., 2002; Teravaninthorn & Raballand, 2009; Fay et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2024).

In this process, capitalist state power, namely the power of capital exercised through the capitalist state, produces infrastructure’s investment value through legal classification, the allocation of concessions, guarantees, and regulatory frameworks. It assumes insurance responsibilities at moments of crisis and redefines infrastructure’s public-good properties in terms of ‘asset security’ and ‘predictable returns’ (O’Neill, 2013; Szabó & Jelinek, 2023; Bakonyi & Darwich, 2025). Within a country, multi-city growth coalitions form around roads, ports, and logistics and industrial parks, capturing rents from trade expansion. Infrastructure consequently advances the local spatial reorganisation of state capitalism and the extraction of profit (Wachsmuth, 2017; Addie et al., 2019).

Capitalist power may also give rise to *infrastructural violence* as a means of exclusion. Mains’s (2012) study of electricity privatisation in Jimma, Ethiopia, shows how privatisation reforms reshape development narratives through unstable electricity supply and new pricing systems. Meehan (2014) develops the concept of ‘*tool-power*’, explaining that water pipelines and metering do more than convey resources. Through administration and billing, they shape obedience and dependence, thereby reproducing the boundaries of state power.

Szabó and Jelinek (2023) examine the Belgrade–Budapest railway and reveal how capital, the state, and local governments negotiate risk sharing and benefit distribution in large projects, highlighting the complex role of infrastructure in reproducing state-capitalist power. The narrative of ‘modernisation’ in port construction, for example, shapes the historical time of cities and states. Symbols such as ‘efficient logistics’ and ‘mutual benefit and win-win cooperation’, together with the actual circulation of capital, strengthen control over territory (Bakonyi & Darwich, 2025; Darwich & Bakonyi, 2025).

Critical political geography further regards infrastructure as embedded in racialised financial capitalism and liberal necropolitics. Through the spatial allocation of resources and risks, capitalist power imposes higher environmental, migration, and security costs on minority groups and places them in conditions of ‘high risk, low protection’ (Horner & Hulme, 2019; Ranganathan, 2019; Sims, 2021; Acosta & Baldomero-Quintana, 2024; Mavelli, 2024).

Reviewing the literature, Zheng (2025) observes that infrastructure projects can transform traditional livelihoods into market-oriented livelihoods. Yet the market is a capitalist market: whoever controls capital controls livelihoods. Colonial infrastructure development in India and Tanzania, for example, completely destroyed existing local industrial and livelihood structures, using infrastructure to maximise the efficiency of resource extraction (Karuka, 2019). The PPP model advocated by neoliberalism now often appears as a national development project designed to legitimise capital accumulation and suppress social contestation at the narrative level (Engel et al., 2013; Fay et al., 2021). Infrastructural violence thereby produces ‘*development through dispossession*’, reshaping local livelihoods and the boundaries of sovereignty.

Roads, dams, and cross-border corridors create regional channels. Through land acquisition, forced relocation (eviction), and debt relations, they can also legitimate the reorganisation of land and labour in the name of development and suppress resistance from those who are harmed (Mains, 2012; Meehan, 2014; Sims, 2021; Lawhon et al., 2023; Kioko & Changwony, 2025; Zheng, 2025). The capitalist power of infrastructure often appears through technical and financial structures, making the identification and interpretation of the relevant institutions and practices indispensable (Star, 1999; Bakonyi et al., 2025; Darwich & Bakonyi, 2025).
