### 1. From the Political Economy of Interests to the Political Economy of Ideas

Modern political economy commonly begins with interests when explaining policy and institutional outcomes. Individuals, firms, industries and social classes are assumed to possess relatively stable material interests, while political competition unfolds around those interests. Industrial groups promote trade protection because tariffs can raise sectoral returns. Affluent groups oppose redistribution because taxation reduces their disposable income. Associated with Stigler, Becker and the subsequent public-choice tradition, this approach has considerable explanatory power in relation to lobbying, rent-seeking, policy capture and the persistence of inefficient institutions.

Mukand and Rodrik begin from a deceptively simple question. If interests can directly explain policy positions, why must political actors continually produce narratives, concepts, symbols and moral arguments? In practice, policy proposals are seldom presented openly as demands serving a particular group. Politicians, business leaders, intellectuals and media organisations usually explain how the world has changed, why a given policy will work, who belongs to the relevant community, and which outcomes accord with fairness, freedom, responsibility or the national interest.

Mukand and Rodrik therefore seek to incorporate ideas into formal political economy as a political force with effects of its own. They connect two intellectual traditions that have largely developed apart. The first is associated with Keynes and Hayek, both of whom emphasised the influence of ideas, knowledge and intellectuals. The second is the Stigler-Becker political economy of interests. Their central proposition is that ideas and interests jointly shape policy and continually reconfigure one another. Interests affect which ideas receive funding, organisational support and channels of dissemination. Ideas, in turn, alter how actors perceive and interpret their interests.

Mukand and Rodrik also engage a longstanding division between economics and the constructivist social sciences. Sociology, political science and international relations have long treated identities, norms and discourses as constitutive forces. Economics has more commonly taken preferences as given and modelled politics as competition among actors holding pre-existing preferences. Mukand and Rodrik show that the political construction of identity and preference can be incorporated into a rational-choice framework. The crucial task is to specify which variable an idea changes and how that change reconfigures policy choice.

### 2. Two Forms of Ideational Politics

Mukand and Rodrik distinguish between *worldview politics* and *identity politics*. Both can transform political equilibrium, though they operate upon different dimensions of political judgement.

Worldview politics changes how actors understand the workings of the world. More precisely, it changes their beliefs about the causal relationship between policies, underlying conditions and expected outcomes. A low-income voter may initially believe that tax reductions principally benefit the affluent and contract the provision of public services. Political actors can offer a different account. Lower taxation raises incentives to invest, investment promotes growth, and growth eventually raises the incomes of ordinary citizens. The voter’s immediate income position remains unchanged, but expectations about policy consequences shift. The voter may consequently reorder the available policy alternatives.

Mukand and Rodrik call the communicative vehicle carrying such an idea a *policy meme*. A meme may take the form of a slogan, a diagram, an analogy, a set of policy terms or a concise causal narrative. It compresses a complex policy problem into a cognitive frame that can be understood and circulated rapidly. The comparison between government finance and household finance, frequently invoked in support of fiscal austerity, is a paradigmatic example. Households cannot indefinitely spend beyond their means. The government, according to this analogy, must also reduce its deficit. The comparison overlooks the distinctive capacities of governments in taxation, monetary policy, borrowing and counter-cyclical management. Its communicative advantages remain substantial. It is familiar, concise and presented as common sense. The political efficacy of an idea therefore depends upon its substantive content, its cognitive accessibility, its narrative economy and its capacity to mobilise everyday experience.

Identity politics changes how actors understand who they are, or increases the salience of a particular identity at a particular political moment. One person may simultaneously be a worker, a low-income citizen, a member of an ethnic group, a religious believer, a regional resident and a national citizen. Different identities can support different political coalitions. When class identity is salient, low-income citizens may support redistribution. When national, ethnic or religious identity becomes salient, the same citizens may enter a cross-class coalition with affluent groups.

An *identity meme* activates a particular identity marker by redefining ‘us’ and ‘them’. Political actors may emphasise divisions between natives and outsiders, majorities and minorities, patriots and traitors, victims and privileged groups. Some low-income voters may then support a policy that reduces their material income because the utility derived from belonging, collective honour or relative group status compensates for the economic loss.

Worldview politics primarily alters beliefs. Identity politics primarily alters salient preferences. The former addresses how the world works. The latter defines who an individual is and who belongs to the same political community. This distinction enables Mukand and Rodrik to incorporate different ideational mechanisms into a common political-economy framework.

### 3. How Political Entrepreneurs Use Ideas to Change Policy Equilibrium

Mukand and Rodrik construct a parsimonious model of electoral competition. Society consists of a low-income majority and a high-income minority. The incumbent represents the low-income majority and favours the status quo policy, \(a_0\). The affluent challenger favours a new policy, \(a_1\).

Policy returns depend upon the underlying state of the world. Under \(S_0\), the status quo policy remains appropriate and the adoption of \(a_1\) harms low-income citizens. Under \(S_1\), the new policy can raise the incomes of both affluent and low-income groups. The affluent support \(a_1\) in either state. Low-income citizens will regard \(a_1\) as materially beneficial only when they believe that the world has moved into \(S_1\).

Under standard median-voter logic, the low-income majority supports the incumbent and \(a_0\). The affluent challenger therefore lacks a natural electoral majority and must alter voters’ judgements through ideational politics. The challenger can invest resources in discovering a policy meme that persuades voters that the underlying state has changed. The challenger may also search for an identity meme that raises the salience of an identity shared with part of the low-income electorate.

Mukand and Rodrik describe this process as the ‘discovery’ of memes. Discovery involves searching for narratives, symbols and policy frames capable of altering political behaviour. The effectiveness of a meme is uncertain and depends upon economic conditions, historical memories, social conflict and communicative capacity. Recession, unemployment, financial crisis or a prolonged history of ethnic conflict may increase public receptivity to particular narratives.

The incumbent can also respond strategically. Measures may include restricting media freedom, intensifying counter-propaganda, raising dissemination costs or repressing the challenger’s organisational networks. Equilibrium therefore depends upon the challenger’s investment in both forms of meme and the incumbent’s efforts to obstruct their dissemination.

Mukand and Rodrik identify four possible configurations. Politics may be dominated by worldview politics, identity politics, or both simultaneously. A fourth possibility arises when neither meme is successfully discovered, in which case established material interests continue to govern political competition.

The significance of this modelling strategy lies in its treatment of ideas as an integral component of political equilibrium. Ideas are not merely cultural background or retrospective rhetoric. They directly alter voters’ perceptions of policy returns and their experiences of political identity.

### 4. Ideational Complementarity and the Recursive Relationship between Ideas and Interests

From the perspective of resource constraints, the two forms of ideational politics compete. Political entrepreneurs possess limited finance and attention. Resources devoted to one type of meme reduce those available for the other. At the level of political returns, however, the relationship may run in the opposite direction. The success of one meme may increase the marginal effectiveness of the other.

Mukand and Rodrik express the sufficient condition for this ideational complementarity in Proposition II as follows:

\[
\frac{
(\mu_1-\mu_0)
\left[
\theta^{10}(S_1)-\theta^{10}(S_0)
\right]
+
(1-2\mu_0)
\left[
g+y^{01}(S_0)
\right]
}{
2\varphi^{I}\varphi^{P}
}
> 0.
\]

When the two preceding gains are sufficiently large relative to \(2\varphi^{I}\varphi^{P}\), this condition holds. Mukand and Rodrik then obtain \(di/dp>0\). A greater likelihood of discovering a policy meme raises the return to investment in an identity meme, and vice versa. The notation and mathematical relationship reproduce Proposition II as stated by Mukand and Rodrik.

The first source of complementarity is the *association effect*. Low-income citizens may derive utility from sharing an identity with a high-status and high-income group. If a new policy further raises the income and social standing of affluent members of that group, poorer members may interpret the group’s success as the success of their own community. A policy meme explains how the policy will strengthen the community. An identity meme encourages individuals to internalise collective success as a personal benefit.

The second source is the *income-identity trade-off*. When mobilisation depends upon identity alone, low-income voters may remain aware that supporting an affluent challenger will reduce their income. A policy meme can attenuate this conflict. Once voters believe that the new policy will generate growth, employment or future income, identity-based support no longer appears to require material sacrifice. The identity narrative gains an economic justification. The economic narrative gains emotional and communal force.

This complementarity helps explain why beliefs about economic policy and identity polarisation frequently appear together. Views concerning taxation, regulation, trade, fiscal austerity or climate change may reinforce national, ethnic, religious or cultural identities. As inequality rises, the political returns to such ideational production may also increase. Affluent groups receive larger potential gains from tax reductions, deregulation and weaker redistribution, which strengthens their incentive to invest in policy narratives. More visible class conflict also increases the value of constructing cross-class identity coalitions. In Mukand and Rodrik, however, inequality remains principally a comparative-static implication rather than a fully developed institutional account.

Ideas and interests consequently form a recursive relationship. Economic interests influence which ideas are produced and disseminated. Ideas alter how actors understand policy consequences and define their interests. Apparent agreement between elites and the wider public may reflect a prolonged process of ideational formation. Interests that appear natural in the present may be the sedimented product of earlier ideas.

### 5. Historical Illustrations and the Capacity of Ideas to ‘Bite’

Mukand and Rodrik use the Laffer Curve and the Reagan tax reductions to show how a policy meme can reframe distributive conflict. The Laffer Curve condenses a complex supply-side argument into a simple relationship. Excessive tax rates may discourage work, investment and tax declaration, meaning that lower rates could increase government revenue. This formulation transformed a policy that disproportionately benefited high-income groups into a general programme for growth. American business elites were not initially united in supporting personal income-tax reductions. Some feared fiscal deficits, inflation and macroeconomic instability. Supply-side economics therefore helped to alter how business actors themselves understood their interests.

The repeal of the Corn Laws illustrates how ideas can redefine the scale at which policy interests are evaluated. In the early 1840s, most British Conservative MPs opposed repeal. Several years later, a substantial number supported free trade. Short-term changes in economic interests provide an incomplete explanation for this shift. Ricardian comparative advantage, the campaigning of the Anti-Corn Law League and Robert Peel’s political leadership jointly reframed the Corn Laws as questions of national prosperity, workers’ living costs and Britain’s international position. Local agricultural interests were thereby situated within a broader conception of the national interest.

Austerity illustrates the political force of everyday analogies and moral language. The claim that governments, like households, cannot indefinitely live beyond their means converted macroeconomic policy into a question of thrift, responsibility and discipline. Complex macroeconomic disagreements were compressed into a conflict between common sense and expert knowledge. Ordinary citizens, experts, prudent households and profligate governments were also organised within an implicit set of identity boundaries. This case leads to Mukand and Rodrik’s dynamic claim that ideas promoted by particular interests can acquire a political life of their own.

British austerity and Brexit illustrate this capacity for ideational ‘bite’. Narratives of fiscal discipline and balanced budgets initially served conservative and financial interests. They also constrained the post-crisis fiscal response and intensified regional hardship and pressure upon public services. The resulting discontent created more favourable conditions for nationalist and anti-immigration mobilisation. Brexit ultimately inflicted serious costs upon London’s financial sector and internationally oriented business groups. Ideas can therefore constrain, and eventually injure, the interests that initially supported their dissemination. Once ideas have changed institutions, public expectations and the boundaries of legitimate policy, their original sponsors cannot fully control their subsequent development.

### 6. Theoretical Contributions and Measured Reservations

Mukand and Rodrik first return ideas to formal political economy. They demonstrate that the political construction of preferences, identities and worldviews can be reconciled with rational-choice modelling. Ideas become concrete mechanisms that alter perceived pay-offs, preference rankings and political coalitions.

Second, the distinction between worldview politics and identity politics improves conceptual precision. Beliefs about policy causation and the salience of identity operate through different channels, yet they may reinforce one another. This helps explain how policies with conspicuous distributive consequences can attract cross-class support. It also helps account for the simultaneous expansion of identity polarisation and particular economic worldviews.

Third, the recursive relationship between ideas and interests extends the conventional causal architecture of political economy. Interests supply the resources and organisational foundations required for ideational production. Ideas change the cognitive frameworks through which interests are interpreted. Once institutionalised, ideas further constrain future policy choices. Mukand and Rodrik summarise this argument in a particularly incisive formulation: ‘Today’s ideas become tomorrow’s interests.’

Three measured reservations remain. First, the production of memes is highly simplified. The specific roles of parties, bureaucracies, media organisations, educational institutions and business associations are compressed into the probability that a political entrepreneur discovers a meme. Second, power is represented principally through resource expenditure and restrictions upon dissemination. Mukand and Rodrik give less attention to deeper and more granular forms of power, including agenda-setting, professional authority, institutional embeddedness and the power to classify social reality. Third, identity is reduced to a single \(B/W\) marker. Actual class, ethnic, religious, regional and occupational identities intersect and are continually recomposed in more complex ways.

When examining policy choice, it remains necessary to ask who benefits. Mukand and Rodrik require political economists to pursue several further questions. How do actors come to believe that they will benefit? Which identities make a particular political coalition possible? Which earlier ideas have hardened into interests that now appear natural and self-evident? These questions constitute the enduring theoretical value of Mukand and Rodrik’s intervention.
