The *strategy-as-practice* (SAP) approach understands strategy as something that persons ‘do’, or as a process of ‘doing’, rather than as an asset or body of knowledge that an organisation ‘has’. SAP takes strategising activities as its primary object of research. Compared with the overuse of abstract concepts such as plans, markets, or resources in mainstream economics and management studies, SAP further investigates the ways agents act and interact in specific situations and draw upon institutional structures; over time, this process creates, stabilises, or changes organisational directionality (Whittington, 1996; Johnson et al., 2007; Vaara & Whittington, 2012; Golsorkhi et al., 2015/2025; Jarzabkowski et al., 2025).

The SAP approach is rooted in the ‘*practice turn*’ in social theory and organisation studies. This turn highlights routinised activities, shared practical understandings, and embodied know-how as the foundations of social order (Giddens, 1984; Bourdieu, 1990; Schatzki, 2002; Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Nicolini, 2012; Golsorkhi et al., 2015/2025). SAP extends this practice turn into strategic management research, tracing the means strategic activities are accomplished in everyday action and connecting micro-fragments of social activities with the long-term development of firms and fields (Langley et al., 2007; Vaara & Lamberg, 2016; Lounsbury et al., 2021).

The historical origins of SAP can be understood as the convergence of two theoretical approaches. One comes from practice theory, which understands social structure as an outcome recursively reproduced through everyday *practices* (Giddens, 1984; Bourdieu, 1990; Schatzki, 2002; cf. Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Althusser, Foucault).

The other comes from processual strategy research on ‘*emergent strategy*’ and strategic change. This revealed the way patterns of action gradually accumulate into realised strategy, but examined the concrete activities and mechanisms involved in those actions in less detail (Burgelman, 1983; Mintzberg & Waters, 1985; Pettigrew, 1992; Hendry & Seidl, 2003). SAP combines these two approaches by integrating insights from practice theory into strategy-process research and focusing attention on *praxis*: the ‘situated doings’ that constitute strategic activity within specific organisational and institutional contexts (Whittington, 1996; Johnson et al., 2003; Langley et al., 2007; Burgelman et al., 2018).

Conceptually, the SAP approach is organised around the categories of *practitioners*, praxis, and practices; subsequent research has extended it to such levels as professional fields. Practitioners are all those who undertake strategy work, including senior managers, middle managers, professionals, consultants, and, at times, external stakeholders. Praxis refers to the process through which strategy is accomplished by a series of concrete activities and strategic episodes, including sequences of strategic meetings, projects, or negotiations that ultimately exert a substantive influence on organisational direction (Hendry & Seidl, 2003; Johnson et al., 2010; Mantere et al., 2012). When dominant frames are challenged, intensive interactions around critical issues can reset a firm’s developmental trajectory, highlighting the temporal and interactional character of strategic practice (Hendry & Seidl, 2003; Johnson et al., 2010; MacKay & Chia, 2013).

Practices are the shared routines, norms, discourses, and knowledge on which practitioners rely when carrying out strategy work, including strategic-planning routines, budgeting processes, and workshops with particular styles or meanings. These practices stabilise particular strategic patterns while also allowing agents, when confronting local pressures, to adapt by recombining or fine-tuning them through local improvisation (Jarzabkowski, 2003, 2004; Jarzabkowski & Spee, 2009; Whittington, 2006; Smets et al., 2015).

SAP examines how these elements interact, for example, how middle managers mobilise established practices during restructuring, or how practice bundles travel and change across locations and levels. In pluralistic settings and among competing institutional structures, SAP research clarifies how practitioners use everyday compromises, bricolage, and sequencing to manage tensions among different institutional demands (Balogun & Johnson, 2004; Mantere, 2005; Denis et al., 2007; Jarzabkowski et al., 2009; Smets et al., 2015).

SAP has developed a practice-theoretical ontology in which ‘practices’ are ‘socially recognisable, materially mediated, and historically sedimented ways of doing and saying that organise what counts as appropriate strategic conduct in a field’ (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Nicolini, 2012; Vaara & Whittington, 2012). Strategic agents are treated as knowledgeable agents whose practical sense is shaped by these practice resources but is always re-enacted in specific situations. This replaces methodological individualism by understanding individual behaviour through practices and examining how different structures jointly ‘enable’ and ‘constrain’ strategic conduct (Whittington, 2006; Vaara & Lamberg, 2016; Lounsbury et al., 2021). In addition, the knowledgeability principle holds that SAP should focus on how embodied, skilful know-how is woven by agents into everyday strategic practices (Rouleau & Cloutier, 2022; Knight et al., 2025; Li & Jarzabkowski, 2025).

For the SAP approach, strategy is jointly enacted by multiple kinds of organisational actors, including actors within organisations, and by agentic organisations. SAP first places its empirical centre on the actual work of strategy practitioners, extending the object of study from a small number of senior managers to diverse groups of practitioners (Whittington, 1996; Jarzabkowski et al., 2025). Narrative perspectives understand strategy as a process of ‘storytelling’, focusing on how strategic discourse narrates identities and power relations, as well as how strategic narratives themselves are constructed, contested, and stabilised (Barry & Elmes, 1997; Fenton & Langley, 2011; Brown & Thompson, 2013; Mantere & Whittington, 2021).

Research on middle management reveals how the many actors below the apex of the organisational pyramid interpret ambiguous and complex change agendas and advance strategic renewal through their own sense-making, narratives, and championing (Floyd & Lane, 2000; Balogun & Johnson, 2004; Rouleau, 2005). In highly uncertain and contested settings, issue-selling, sense-giving, and local experimentation constitute the content and implementation process that shape strategic change (Balogun & Johnson, 2004; Mantere, 2005; Mantere & Whittington, 2021).

Johnson et al. (2007) and Langley et al. (2007) define SAP as an *activity-based view of strategy*, distinguish it from traditional perspectives on micro-level strategy content or macro-level structures, and ask: who engages in strategic practice, what practices are involved, and what consequences do these practices produce? Taking **practitioners, praxis, and practices** as its core, Jarzabkowski and Spee (2009) argue that the interactions and relationships among the three dimensions must be studied rather than treating any one dimension in isolation. Together, this research shifts attention from strategic outcomes towards strategising as situated processes.

Further, Vaara and Whittington (2012) connect SAP to practice theory and institutional theory, arguing that strategic practices are sociohistorically situated and calling for research that links the organisational and societal levels. Burgelman et al. (2018) propose that strategy processes and strategy practices should be viewed as two sides of the same phenomenon. Research on historical embeddedness and future-making shows that strategic practices are profoundly shaped by inherited historical legacies and imaginaries of possible futures (Vaara & Lamberg, 2016; Wenzel, 2022; Jarzabkowski et al., 2025).

Other studies introduce SAP into discussions of institutions and fields, examining how local strategic practices both reproduce and shape broader norms and rules (Smets et al., 2015; Lounsbury et al., 2021). Between pluralistic organisations and competing goals and institutional expectations, agents often reach temporary compromises (Denis et al., 2007; Jarzabkowski et al., 2009; Smets et al., 2015; Lounsbury et al., 2021). In critical interactions, changes in frames and structures may reverse or accelerate strategic change, making the detail of concrete micro-events crucial to understanding an overall trajectory (Hendry & Seidl, 2003; Samra-Fredericks, 2003; Laine & Vaara, 2007; Carter et al., 2008; Johnson et al., 2010; Vaara et al., 2010; Mantere et al., 2012; MacKay & Chia, 2013).

As Prashantham and Healey (2022) and Jarzabkowski et al. (2025) reflect through comparisons between SAP and mainstream strategic-management research, SAP’s context-sensitive and historically embedded methodology can reveal the complex, dynamic, and consequential everyday strategy work underlying phenomena such as cross-border collaboration, future-oriented strategic practice, and ecosystem construction in pluralistic settings: the ways in which strategy is ‘being done’ (Vaara & Whittington, 2012; Burgelman et al., 2018; Lounsbury et al., 2021). In summary, SAP can explain the microfoundations of strategic change while remaining sensitive to historical institutions, seeking to explain how local activities both draw upon and reshape institutions and orders (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Seidl & Whittington, 2014; Lounsbury et al., 2021; Li & Jarzabkowski, 2025).

Of course, several problems in the current research remain to be resolved. First, research needs to clarify which activities are strategic activities and how they are related to outcomes/performance. Rouleau and Cloutier (2022) argue that some studies conducted under the SAP label do not genuinely engage with social practice theory; Seidl et al. (2024) point out that ‘strategy’ is frequently overused as a label (Carter et al. 2008). Accordingly, Burgelman et al. (2018), Seidl et al. (2024), and Jarzabkowski et al. (2025) call for clearer criteria for defining what ‘*strategicity*’ actually is. They seek to integrate organisational direction, scope, and advantage with research findings on sense-making, language, socio-materiality, institutions, and processes (Kohtamäki et al. 2022; ; Golsorkhi et al., 2015/2025), and to develop a systematic framework explaining the mechanisms that connect observable activities, strategic episodes, and generation with changes in organisational directionality and capability-building (Li & Jarzabkowski, 2025).

Second, research needs to strengthen the *micro-macro linkage* and embed it in institutional theory. Vaara and Whittington (2012), Vaara and Lamberg (2016), and Lounsbury et al. (2021) emphasise the need to reveal how local strategic practices both reflect and reshape fields and social structures, calling for historical comparative research across sectors, regions, and organisations. According to the recent reflection by Li and Jarzabkowski (2025), the radical impulse with which SAP initially addressed power relations and inequalities in strategic practice was weakened as the literature expanded. They therefore call for the reintroduction of a more critical stance, including broader participants such as consumers, employees, and the natural environment, both human and non-human (Jarzabkowski et al., 2025), and for questioning the strategic knowledge that dominates under capitalism.

Third, SAP should be extended to settings such as *digitalisation and grand challenges*. Research on online communities and complex collaboration demonstrates the application of SAP to digital and social issues (Reischauer & Mair, 2018; Wenzel, 2022; Jarzabkowski et al., 2025; Knight et al., 2025). Future research can further analyse algorithms, platforms, virtual strategic episodes, and community sense-making, thereby reshaping the nature of agency in strategy work: the question of who and what.
